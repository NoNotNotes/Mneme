# Shock
Shock is a condition characterized by **profound hemodynamic and metabolic disturbance due to inadequate blood flow and oxygen delivery** to the capillaries and tissues of the body. [^1]Most frequently, this manifests as a result of hypotension.
1. **Hypovolemic shock** appears when an individual loses 10% to 15% of their total blood volume, such as from bleeding, dehydration (diarrhea, vomiting, gastric lavage, diaphoresis, diuresis), burns, trauma, and ascites.
2. **Cardiogenic shock** occurs with the failure of the heart to maintain cardiac output, such as in cases of congestive heart failure, acute coronary syndrome (myocardial infarction), arrhythmias, cardiac tamponade, etc.
3. **Distributive shock** occurs when systemic vasodilation shunts blood away from vital organs. This process may appear through various conditions:
	- **Septic shock**, also known as **toxic shock**, occurs when infection (especially by gram-negative organisms) produces a systemic inflammatory response with endotoxins, producing vasodilation and allowing the leakage of fluid into interstitial spaces.
	- **Anaphylactic shock**, occurs when allergens trigger the release of histamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes and prostaglandins that result in vasodilation.
	- **Neurogenic shock**, also called spinal shock, occurs from generalized vasodilatation due to conditions affecting the medulla oblongata and sympathetic nervous system, e.g., spinal cord injury, head injury, general anesthesia, and overdosing on opiates, barbiturates, or tranquilizers.
	- **Burns** may also cause shock by causing vasodilation, in addition to fluid loss.
4. **Obstructive shock** occurs when a blockage results in deficient blood flow, such as in cases of pulmonary embolism.
## Stages of Shock
During the initial stage of shock, compensatory mechanisms activate that allow for mitigation of the signs of shock. This is known as the **compensated stage**, also known as the **non-progressive stage**. Compensatory mechanisms induce vasoconstriction, tachycardia, and tachypnea. These allow for the transient maintenance of adequate blood perfusion.
1. **Sympathy-Adreno-Medullary Response** (SAMR): the mechanism behind the release of epinephrine and norepinephrine which have positive inotropic, positive chronotropic, and vasoconstrictive effects on the cardiovascular system.
	- Vasoconstriction in the liver can increase circulating fluid volume by ~350 mL.
	- Hyperventilation from sympathetic nervous system stimulation can increase oxygenation to tissues, but also poses the risk of producing respiratory alkalosis.
2. **Antidiuretic Hormone** (ADH) release promotes water retention and helps conserve blood volume. It causes arteriolar vasoconstriction and helps maintain blood pressure.
3. **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System** (RAAS): the kidneys detect decreased blood pressure, and react by producing renin, which interact with angiotensinogen to form angiotensin (a potent vasoconstrictor), and release aldosterone (a salt-saving, fluid-retaining hormone).

During the compensated stage, clinical manifestations include:
1. [^2]**Blood pressure**: normal or slightly elevated (overcompensation).
2. **Urine output**: normal, 0.5–1.0 mL/kg/hr in adults, 1–2 mL/kg/hr in infants. Approximately 30–60 mL/hour in adults.
3. [^3]**Bowel sounds**: normal bowel sounds, 5–30 sounds/min. Borborygmi
4. **Peripherals**: warm, dry skin with normal capillary refill time (≤2 seconds). Pulses are of normal [^4]grade (+2).
5. Other manifestations include thirst, anxiety, restlessness, confusion, and dizziness.

As shock continues unmanaged, decompensation occurs. Decompensation can occur through the exhaustion of the body's physiologic reserves that maintain homeostasis, or worsening of the etiology of shock to a degree that cannot be corrected by the body. This is known as the **decompensated stage** or the **progressive phase**. Organ perfusion continues to deteriorate in this stage, and shunting of blood to core organs (lungs, heart, and brain) attempt to maintain vital functions. Blood is shunted from the skin, gastrointestinal tract, liver, spleen, and skeletal muscles. Clinical manifestations include:
1. The **classic triad for shock** manifests in this stage: hypotension, tachycardia, and tachypnea.
	- Neurogenic shock manifests hypotension, bradycardia, and bradypnea.
2. **Urine output**: oliguria, <30 mL/hr or <400 ml/day. Specific gravity is high (normally 1.010–1.030).
3. **Bowel sounds**: hypoactive due to shunting, which can result in constipation and [^5]fecal impaction which, if unmanaged, can lead to perforation, bleeding, and infection.
4. **Peripherals**: cool to touch, moist/clammy skin. Capillary refill is delayed (>2 seconds). Nail beds are cyanotic. Pulse grading is weak (+1).
5. There may be an **altered level of consciousness** as cerebral perfusion becomes inadequate. Varying degrees of impairment may be present, from lethargy to disorientation. In severe cases, psychosis may occur.

>[!WARNING] Multiple Organ Dysfunction Syndrome
>1. The kidneys experience acute tubular necrosis and renal failure.
>2. The brain undergoes hypoxia and accumulates of toxic substances. Acidosis also occurs from anaerobic metabolism. All of these result in an altered level of consciousness.
>3. The heart's contractility and output decreases. Dysrhythmias and cardiac arrest may occur.
>4. The lungs may experience acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), leading to atelectasis and pulmonary edema.
>5. The gastrointestinal tract is paralyzed. Colonic microorganisms may be lysed and release endotoxins and result in septic shock. Necrosis of intestinal mucosa can also result in occult to massive bleeding.
>6. The liver's Kupffer cells are destroyed, which allows bacteria to enter general circulation and cause septic shock. Metabolic function is also decreased.
>7. Disseminated intravascular coagulation may occur.
>
>>[!TIP] All types of shock have a septic component due to #5 and #6!

The terminal stage of shock can resolve either as the **irreversible stage**, which eventually leads to death, or the **refractory stage**, where shock resolves. In irreversible shock, marked hypoperfusion results in multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).
1. The triad of symptoms begin to fail, producing **hypotension, bradycardia, and bradypnea**.
2. **Urine output**: anuria, <100 mL/hr. Acute kidney injury may occur from ischemia and necrosis. Filtration fails, allowing blood and proteins to enter the urine. Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is lower than 90 mL/min/1.73 m². Creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) becomes elevated. Flank pain may be present.
3. **Bowel sounds**: paralytic ileus sets in due to ischemia. No bowel sounds are audible. This mandates placing the patient on absolute NPO status due to the heightened aspiration risk.
4. **Peripherals**: cool to touch, moist/clammy skin. Pulse is non-palpable; a doppler ultrasound may detect pulse. Nail beds may be necrotized (black). Amputation may be required in prolonged hypoperfusion.
5. Ischemia produces multiple problems:
	- **Myocardial infarction** can occur from hypoxia of the cardiac muscles. This is marked by the release of the Troponin I cardiac enzyme.
	- **Hyperkalemia** from cell destruction releasing potassium into the blood can increase the risk for cardiac dysrhythmias.
	- **Anaerobic metabolism**, the body's reserve method of metabolism in the absence of oxygen, produces lactic acid as a byproduct. In severe, prolonged periods of hypoxia, accumulation of lactic acid can result in metabolic acidosis that can further injure the heart, lungs, and kidneys.

>[!EXAMPLE] Hemoconcentration in Hypovolemic Shock
>**Hemoconcentration** is an increase in the concentration of blood components, is a common outcome in hypovolemic shock resulting from fluid depletion (diarrhea, vomiting, dehydration). All of the following become elevated.
>- **Serum osmolality** is normally 275–300 mOsm/kg
>- **Serum sodium concentration** is normally 135 to 145 mEq/L
>- **Blood urea nitrogen** is normally 8 to 20 mg/dL
>- **Hemoglobin** is normally approximately 12 to 16 g/dL for females and 14 to 18 g/dL for males.
>- **Hematocrit** in normal patients can be clinically approximated by multiplying hemoglobin by 3—it is normally 36% to 48% for females and 42% to 54% for males.
>
>>[!TIP] If blood loss occurs, hemoglobin and hematocrit will go down proportionally.
>
>**Hemodynamic parameters** are all decreased in hypovolemic shock as they exist in proportion to blood volume.
>- **Cardiac output** (CO) is the volume of blood pumped by the heart in 60 seconds. This ranges from 4 to 9 liters per minute.
>- **Blood pressure** (BP) defined by the AHA is *normal* from 90/60 mm Hg up to 119/79 mm Hg and *elevated blood pressure* from 120/80 mm Hg to 129/80 mm Hg. According to the WHO, hypertension denotes a blood pressure greater than 140/90 mm Hg.
>- **Central venous pressure** (CVP) is measured by placing a sensor in the superior vena cava. It should normally be 2 to 8 mm Hg.
>- **Mean arterial pressure** (MAP) is the average pressure in the arteries. It ranges from 70 to 100 mm Hg. It can be [^6]calculated by dividing pulse pressure by 3 and adding it to the diastolic pressure.
>
>>[!TIP] Monitoring may require invasive procedures (e.g., radial artery catheter, CVP monitoring)

## Management of Hypovolemic Shock

1. **Airway**: use the head tilt chin lift or [^7]jaw thrust maneuver.
	- An **endotracheal tube** may be inserted if necessary. Otherwise, a **tracheostomy** may be required.
	- Place the patient in the **recovery position**—left lateral position with flexed right knee. This protects and maintains the airway.
2. **Breathing**: use a bag-valve mask to delivery oxygen.
3. **Circulation**: establish two intravenous access sites using large-bore needles (gauge #14–#16). If intravenous access is difficult to establish, an intraosseous site may be used. Intraosseous access also provides rapid administration due to the vascular nature of marrow. These access points will allow for the initiation of fluid volume resuscitation and delivery of medications.
	- If bleeding is a major concern, apply pressure to actively bleeding sites. If there is no IV line available (e.g., in the pre-hospital setting), a tourniquet can be applied to the extremity as a last resort. The use of a tourniquet can result in greater damage from ischemic limb injury or reperfusion injury. Tourniquets also should not be loosened in the field due to the risk of complications from rhabdomyolysis.
	- A central access also allows for rapid administration, and may be used in the hospital setting.
4. **Positioning**: a modified Trendelenburg position—head flat, legs elevated—allow for faster venous return to improve perfusion to vital organs.
5. **Fluid Resuscitation** prior to the administration of a vasoconstrictor uses crystalloids and [^8]plain normal saline solution.
	- Inversely, the patient is positioned upright with the legs dangling in the event of a fluid overload.
6. **Vasopressors** (e.g., norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, dobutamine, vasopressin) constrict and press fluids back into circulation to elevate blood pressure. These are only used if fluids are inadequate in correcting hypotension.
	- Antihypertensives are contraindicated in shock due to hypotension.
7. **Blood Transfusion** is indicated for severe hemorrhage. Packed red blood cells are transfused as [^9]targeted replacement therapy.
	- Transfusion is done via a gauge #14–#16 access.
	- Normal saline is the most common fluid used in conjunction with blood transfusion therapy.
8. **Monitoring**:
	- Vital signs every 5 to 15 minutes
	- Strict intake and output
	- Weight, a reliable indicator of fluid balance.

## Starting an Intravenous Infusion

1. **Verify the order for venipuncture**.
2. **Confirm patient identity**. Commonly, at least two identification methods are used. In order of reliability, the nurse: (1) checks the identification bracelet/band, (2) instructs the patient to state their name and birthdate, and (3) verifies identity with the staff member.
3. **Assess for contraindications**, e.g., allergies, arteriovenous fistula (preserved for hemodialysis access), [^10]mastectomy site (risk for lymphedema), hemiplegia, infection, phlebitis, and areas below previous infiltrations or extravasations.
4. **Perform hand hygiene** and observe other appropriate infection prevention procedures.
5. **Prepare equipment and paraphernalia**.
6. **Perform hand hygiene** again before initiating client contact.
7. **Select the venipuncture site**:
	- Use the nondominant arm unless contraindicated. Identify possible venipuncture sites by looking for relatively straight veins. They may be palpable, but may not be visible especially in clients with dark skin.
	- Consider the catheter length; look for a site sufficiently distal to the wrist or elbow such that the tip of the catheter will not be at a point of flexion.

>[!TIP] Principles
>In **adults**, the dorsal aspect of the hands at the arteriovenous arch is the preferred site of insertion. Site selection for **infants** is preferably the feet or scalp.
>- If using the hand, use the **non-dominant** hand.
>- **Distal** sites are preferred over proximal sites.
>- Avoid mastectomy sites and other contraindications such as comprised circulation and injury.
>- Insert on areas **naturally splinted by bone**; avoid mobile areas—areas of flexion. The underlying bone prevents joint movement, keeping the IV more stable and less prone to dislodgement.
>- Easily ***palpable* veins** are better than *highly visible* veins.

8. **Dilate the vein**.
	- Place the extremity in a dependent position.
	- Apply a tourniquet 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in.) above the venipuncture site. It should be tight enough to obstruct venous flow, but not too tight as to obstruct arterial flow. Palpate for a radial pulse to assess the adequacy of arterial flow. Tourniquet use may not be appropriate for clients with fragile skin (e.g., older adults).
	- If the vein is not sufficiently dilated, (a) massage or stroke the vein distal to the site and in the direction of venous flow toward the heart; (b) encourage the client to clench and unclench the fist; (c) lightly tap the vein with your fingertips.
	- If the vein remains unpalpable, remove the tourniquet and wrap the extremity in a warm towel for 10 to 15 minutes. This dilates superficial blood vessels, causing them to fill.
	- If no veins are visible or easily palpated, use vein visualization technology to improve insertion success.
	- Remove the tourniquet once a site has been selected for preparation of the site.
9. **Apply gloves and clean the venipuncture site**. Soap and water if visibly soiled, then with chlorhexidine applied in a [^11]circulation motion from the site outwards.
10. **Insert the catheter and initiate the infusion**.
11. **Stabilize the catheter and apply a dressing**.
	- Apply a **transparent semipermeable membrane** (TSM) over the insertion site. Transparency allows for continuous assessment of the site. No ointments of any kind is placed under the dressing. No tape should be placed directly on the TSM dressing.
	- Label the dressing with the date and time of insertion, gauge, and initials.
12. **Ensure appropriate infusion flow**. An arm board may be used to splint the joint if needed. Adjust the infusion rate of flow according to the order.
13. **Label** the tubing, and bag with the date and time of insertion as basis for dressing (labeled earlier), tubing, and bag changes at regular intervals, i.e., according to agency policy. Tubing and bag changes may be done every 1 to 2 days and dressing changes done every 3 to 4 days.
14. **Perform client teaching**: limitations on movement and activity, protection of the site, signs and symptoms of complications, and alarms if an electronic infusion device is being used. Importantly, emphasize infection prevention precautions, including hand hygiene by all healthcare providers who provide care.
15. **Document** all assessments and interventions.
16. **Regularly check** the client for intended and adverse effects of the infusion.
## Complications of Intravenous Therapy
1. **Infiltration**: leakage of fluid to surrounding tissues. This manifests as cool, clammy skin and pallor.
2. **Phlebitis**: inflammation of the vein, which can be caused by [^12]multiple attempts of insertion. This manifests as warmth, redness, pain, and swelling. The narrowing of the veins will lead to clot formation, resulting in the development of *thrombophlebitis*.
 3. **Thrombophlebitis**: warmth, redness, pain, and swelling. This may occur as sequelae to phlebitis. A differentiating sign between phlebitis and thrombophlebitis is an **engorged cord-like vein**. This can result in complications related to embolism: cardiovascular accident, myocardial infarction, and pulmonary embolisms. Another problem with clot formation is occlusion of the intravenous line. Patency can be assessed by lowering the intravenous fluid bag to allow for backflow. The presence of backflow indicates patency.
4. **Extravasation**: a type of infiltration involving vesicants (e.g., chemotherapeutic agents, potassium chloride, antibiotics, vasopressors) entering surrounding tissues. This manifests as warmth, redness, pain, blister and ulcer formation, and necrosis (eschar formation).
5. **Fluid Volume Overload** occurs when fluid is infused in large amounts or infused too rapidly. This can manifest as hypertension, tachycardia, tachypnea, jugular vein distention, and pulmonary edema (rales, crackles, pink frothy sputum).
	- Jugular vein distention is assessed by first placing the patient in a semi-Fowler's position, i.e., jugular vein distention in a patient who is supine may be normal. The degree of distention is quantified by measuring the height of
	- In mild cases when no signs of respiratory distress has appeared, the infusion is slowed to a keep-vein-open rate—around 10 to 20 gtts/min or 10 mL/hr.
	- In moderate cases when respirations become affected, the infusion is stopped entirely, and the patient is positioned upright (sitting) with the legs dangling.
6. **Infection**: warmth, redness, pain, purulent discharge, fever, and leukocytosis. Confirmation of infection is done via culture.

>[!TIP] Interventions
>Management of infusion reactions generally follow the same set of interventions:
>1. **Stop the infusion**.
>2. **Elevate the extremity**.
>3. **Apply warm** (for swelling) **or cold** (for pain) **compresses**.
>4. In most complications, **the intravenous catheter is removed**. However, in cases of **extravasation**, the catheter is *kept in place* as it can act as access to the site for aspirating leakage or administering an antidote.
>5. **Notify the health care provider**.
>6. **Reinsert IV at different site**.
>7. An **incident report** (IR) is made when any complications occurs.

## Types of Intravenous Solutions
1. **Crystalloids** are intravenous fluids composed of water and small solutes such as electrolytes (e.g., sodium, chloride, potassium) and sometimes glucose. They **do not contain proteins or large molecules**, so they easily move across semi-permeable membranes (between intravascular and interstitial compartments). Crystalloids are the first-line choice for fluid resuscitation in most clinical settings due to their availability, low cost, and lower risk of allergic reactions.
 2. **Colloids** are intravenous solutions that contain large molecules, such as proteins or polysaccharides, which stay in the intravascular space and draw fluid into the bloodstream via oncotic pressure. These are often referred to as **protein or volume expanders**. Examples include: albumin, dextran, Hetastarch, blood products, and plasma expanders.
3. **Isotonic solutions** are of equal concentration to the body's fluids. stays in the circulation - the best solution for fluid replacement in patients with blood loss.
	1. [^16]**Normal saline solution** (0.9% sodium chloride solution) is the most commonly used intravenous fluid. It is used for blood transfusion, irrigation during on going surgery, wound care, shock, and as diluent for potassium chloride.
	 2. [^17]**Lactated Ringer's Solution** (Ringer's Lactate Solution) contains electrolytes—sodium, chloride, potassium, calcium—and lactate. It is often used for [^15]burns and metabolic acidosis (lactate is alkaline).
	 3. **5% Dextrose in Water** (D5W) contains glucose, the primary source of energy for the body. This solution is isotonic when stored, but later becomes hypotonic once the dextrose has been utilized by the body. This can be used in patients with hypernatremia as it does not contain sodium.
	4. **Normosol** is an alternative electrolyte solution used for fluid resuscitation and replacement in cases of dehydration, shock, or surgery. It contains sodium, potassium, magnesium, chloride, and acetate, which is metabolized to bicarbonate, helping buffer acidosis.
	5. **Plasmalyte** is designed to mimic the composition of plasma. It contains sodium, potassium, magnesium, chloride, acetate, and gluconate. These components help maintain acid-base balance and are useful in situations requiring large-volume fluid replacement, such as trauma, surgery, or sepsis. It is also compatible with blood products.
4. **Hypotonic solutions** are dilute in comparison to the body's fluids. This is the state of many solvents and water due to the abundance of solutes in the body. Introduction of hypotonic solutions into the body create [^13]**cell swelling effects**. As such, it is used for patients experiencing cellular dehydration, the [^14]most severe form of all dehydration. However, hypotonic solutions are not given to patients with fluid volume deficit (hypovolemia). Hypotonic fluids do not stay in the intravascular compartment of the body and into the cells, which can result in cerebral edema. They also shift water into cells, which can worsen low blood pressure.
	- Any saline or dextrose solution less concentrated than 0.9% saline and 5% dextrose is hypotonic. Additionally, as mentioned, 5% dextrose itself is considered hypotonic when infused into the body.
	- Hypotonic solutions may be given to patients with renal failure to dilute the accumulation of waste products in the body.
5. **Hypertonic solutions** are concentrated in comparison to the body's fluids. These draw fluids out of the body's cells—exert a **cell shrinking effect**.
	- Any saline or dextrose solution more concentrated than 0.9% saline and 5% dextrose is hypotonic. This includes combinations of dextrose with other solutions such as D5LR and D5NSS.
	- Due to its cell shrinking effects, hypertonic solutions are not given to patients with fluid volume overload (e.g., syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone, pulmonary edema, congestive heart failure, Cushing's disease, renal failure) and for patients who are experiencing cell dehydration.
	- **Dextrose 10% in Water** (D10W) can be used as an alternative for total parenteral nutrition to prevent hypoglycemia. In severe cases (hypoglycemic coma), **D50W** is given via central line as management.

>[!EXAMPLE] Osmosis
>A **solution** is a mixture of solvent and solutes. A **solvent** is the fluid component of a solution. **Solutes** are substances dissolved into a solution. The universal solvent, and the primary solvent in blood, is water. This makes up 90–92% of plasma. It dissolves and transports various solutes: electrolytes, proteins, gases (O₂, CO₂), nutrients, waste products, hormones, and drugs.
>
>The most prominent form of fluid transport is **osmosis**. This involves the movement of fluid from compartments of low concentration to compartments with high concentration through a semi-permeable membrane. Concentration is based on the density of solutes in a solution.
>
>A condition that exemplifies the effects of osmosis is *diabetes mellitus*. Once hyperglycemia breaches the renal threshold (~180 mg/dL), glycosuria occurs. Glucose in the urine exerts an osmotic force, attracting more fluid into the urine, which results in polyuria, the osmotic sign of DM. If dehydration occurs, this results in polydipsia, an osmotic symptom of DM.

# Blood Transfusion
A **blood transfusion** is the introduction of **whole blood** or other **blood components** into venous circulation. It is used to restore blood volume and the oxygen carrying capacity of the blood.

>[!EXAMPLE] Classifications of Blood Transfusion by Source
>1. **Autologous**: a transfusion from one's self to themself if the requirement for blood can be expected, such as for scheduled surgeries.
>		- While generally still not an accepted by [^19]Jehovah's witnesses, this may be allowed by some as an exception. [^18]Blood, if stored outside the body, even if autologous, is not permitted.
>2. **Allogeneic**/**Homologous**: a transfusion from other human donors.
>3. **Heterologous**: a transfusion from other species—no longer practiced.

## Blood Typing
Blood type is based on the presence of **antigens** on the surface of the red blood cells. There are three main surface antigens in red blood cells: antigen A, antigen B, and the Rhesus factor (Rh).

Similarly, antibodies are formed for antigens A, B, and D (Rhesus factor). Type A and B antibodies in the plasma are present even without prior exposure to the A and B antigens. However, Rh (D) antibodies are only produced by an Rh-negative individual once they have been exposed to an Rh (D) antigen.

| Blood Type      | Surface Antigen on RBCs   | Antibodies in Plasma        |
| --------------- | ------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Type A          | Has B Antigen             | Antibody B                  |
| Type B          | Has B Antigen             | Antibody A                  |
| Type AB         | Has both A and B Antigens | No antibodies               |
| Type O          | Neither A or B Antigens   | Both A and B Antibodies     |
| Rh (D) Positive | Has Rh (D) Antigen        | No Rh (D) Antibody          |
| Rh (D) Negative | No Rh (D) Antigen         | Can be sensitized to Rh (D) |

>[!EXAMPLE] Pretransfusion Compatibility Testing
>Blood is safe to transfuse as long as the recipients has no antibodies against the blood to be given. The following apply:
>1. **ABO Compatibility**:
>		- **Matching letters** are compatible, i.e., A to A, B to B, AB to AB, and O to O.
>		- O is the **universal donor**, as the cells have no antigens. This blood is compatible to any blood type.
>		- AB-type patients are **universal recipients**, as their plasma has no antibodies. They can receive any blood type.
>2. **Rh compatibility** uses the same concept:
>		- Rh-negative blood, which has no antigens, can be given to both Rh-negative and Rh-positive clients.
>		- Rh-positive blood cannot be given to Rh-negative clients, whose bodies will form antibodies against the Rh (D) antigen.
>
>Both types of compatibility must be taken into consideration when transfusing to a client. Below is an illustration of blood type compatibilities by [OpenMD](https://openmd.com/guide/blood-types):

## Blood Transfusion Safety Protocol
1. Verify the physician's orders with the blood product.
2. Match the blood product with the patient's identification.
3. Verify the blood product with another registered nurse.
4. Prepare transfusion equipment:
	- A Y-tubing with a filter has two lines, one for blood and the other for normal saline.
	- Prime the tubing with NSS. Blood is not used for priming to avoid wastage.
	- Intravenous access is established with gauges #14 (largest) to #22 (smallest). Usually, gauge #18 is used.
5. A medical technician performs blood typing and cross matching.
6. Prepare the blood for transfusion:
	- Check the blood product for hemolysis, discoloration, or bubbling.
	- Obtain baseline vital signs. This includes the main vital signs in addition to lung sounds, urine output, and weight.
7. Prepare pre-transfusion medications: used to mitigate transfusion reactions
	- Acetaminophen, an antipyretic, mitigates febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reactions.
	- Diphenhydramine, an antihistamine, mitigates mild allergic transfusion reactions.
	- Hydrocortisone, a glucocorticoid, suppresses immune and inflammatory responses. This is used for severe allergic or anaphylactic reactions.
8. Begin transfusion:
	- Blood products must begin transfusion within 30 minutes of being received. When warmed to room temperature, the risk of bacterial proliferation increases.
	- All blood products must finish transfusion within 4 hours to prevent bacterial proliferation. Prolonged exposure to ambient temperature can also result in hemolysis and reduced efficacy of transfusion therapy.
	- Fresh frozen plasma should begin transfusion within 30 to 60 minutes. When warmed to room temperature, clotting factors degrade quickly. This results in reduced efficacy and increased risk of bacterial contamination.
	- Cryoprecipitate is extremely temperature sensitive and lacks preservatives. It should begin transfusion immediately (within 15 to 30 minutes) after thawing to preserve clotting factor potency.
	- Platelets are constantly agitated to prevent clumping. It is also more prone to bacterial contamination than red cells or plasma. Platelets should be transfused quickly after insurance to minimize infection risk and maintain platelet function. It should also begin transfusion within 15 to 30 minutes per unit to ensure maximum viability and clinical efficacy.
9. Observe for transfusion reactions:
	- Start the transfusion at a keep-vein-open (KVO) rate (≤2 mL/min) to mitigate any potential reactions in the first 15 to 30 minutes of transfusion.
	- Monitor vital signs that vary from the baseline. Vital signs are obtained every 15 minutes for the first hour, then hourly afterwards. Another set of vital signs is obtained after the transfusion is completed.
## Blood Products
1. **Whole blood** contains all components of blood—plasma, white blood cells, platelets, clotting factors, antibodies, and red blood cells (among others). This is not usually used as a blood product due to risk of volume overload, presence of unnecessary components, increased risk of reactions, and shorter period of viability. It may be used for massive hemorrhage or trauma and emergency or rural settings.
2. **Packed red blood cells** (PRBC) are given for blood loss, anemia, and shock.
	- Generally, every unit of PRBC will increase hemoglobin by ~1 g/dL and hematocrit by 3% in stable adults.
3. **Platelet concentrate** is used for thrombocytopenia (<50,000/mm³) and bleeding tendencies (e.g., dengue, leukemia).
	- Generally, every unit of platelet concentration will increase platelet count by 30,000–60,000/mm³. Each unit contains 4–6 donor units; each donor contributes 5,000–10,000/mm³.
4. **Fresh frozen plasma** contains clotting factors produced in the liver. This may be given in cases of liver disease or [^20]disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).
5. **Cryoprecipitate** is a product with more concentrated clotting factors and is the first choice for patients with DIC, and disorder involving clotting factors ([^21]Hemophilia, von Willebrand's).
## Transfusion Reactions
1. **Febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reaction** (FNHTR): a common and (at times) expected reaction. This manifests as fever, chills, rigors, malaise, and anxiety. This occurs due to the lysis of donor leukocytes by recipient antibodies, which releases pro-inflammatory cytokines.
2. **Hemolytic transfusion reaction** occurs due to ABO incompatibility (acute, occurs within minutes to 24 hours) or [^22]other incompatibilities (delayed, occurs 3 to 14 days post-transfusion). This is the most fatal reaction.
	1. **Acute reactions** are the most severe and preventable type. Recipient antibodies bind to donor RBC antigens and lead to intravascular hemolysis. This results in fever and chills from cytokine release, **flank or back pain**, dark urine (**hemoglobinuria**), hypotension, tachycardia, and other positive hemolysis markers.
	2. **Delayed reactions** occur in previously sensitized patients whose immune systems detect antigens and mount an attack once re-exposed. In this case, RBCs are destroyed in extravascular compartments (i.e., mainly in the spleen and liver). This results in a mild fever or jaundice, and a drop in hemoglobin and hematocrit.
	3. The release of hemoglobin (large proteins) by hemolysis, in large amounts, can result in acute kidney injury. This manifests as hemoglobinuria, proteinuria, and flank/lumbar pain.
	4. The release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in large amounts can result in systemic vasodilation severe enough to result in distributive shock.

>[!EXAMPLE] Memory Tool
>Manifestations of a hemolytic reaction include the following (mn. *FEVER HURTS*):
>1. Fever
>2. Elevated HR
>3. Vomiting
>4. Excruciating flank and chest pain
>5. Red urine
>6. Hypotension
>7. Urinay output
>8. Respiratory distress
>9. Thrombosis (DIC)
>10. Shock

3. **Mild allergic transfusion reaction** manifests as **rashes** (urticaria) and **itchiness** (pruritus). This is also a common and expected side effect. It is usually managed with an antihistamine, such as diphenhydramine.
4. **Anaphylactic transfusion reaction** is a severe allergic reaction. The client manifests bronchoconstriction (wheezing) which can lead to airway obstruction (stridor). Anaphylaxis also results in distributive shock—weak pulses, cool and clammy skin, [^23]cyanosis, hypotension, tachycardia, and tachypnea.
	- Hypoxia results in restlessness (the earliest manifestation).
	- [^24]**Angioedema**, edema of the deeper tissues, occurs as massive histamine release produces increased capillary permeability and vasodilation. This typically appears around the lips, tongue, periorbital area, larynx, pharynx, and face. This is dangerous if edema affects the larynx and results in airway obstruction.
	- **Jaundice** appears due to bilirubin formation from hemolysis. The earliest sign of jaundice is often visible at the palate.
	- The drug of choice for management of anaphylaxis is **epinephrine**. The best route for administration is endotracheal, to directly target the airway. According to the World Allergy Organization (WAO) guidelines, 0.3–0.5 mL of 1:1,000 epinephrine is delivered into the [^26]anterolateral thigh [^25]every 5 to 15 minutes for as many doses as necessary\* until advanced airway support can be established.
		- An epinephrine autoinjector—an EpiPen—is a medical device that delivers doses of epinephrine in cases of anaphylaxis. Administration is straight-forward, and follows the phrase "Orange to the thigh, blue to the sky." Injection can be done through clothing. Once activated, the injector is held in place for 10 seconds. One dose may not be effective, and another dose can be given.
5. **Transfusion Associated Circulatory Overload** (TACO) occurs when blood is transfused too quickly or in large amounts. This results in acute pulmonary edema (dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles on auscultation, and hypoxemia), hypertension, tachycardia, and jugular vein distention.
6. **Blood-borne pathogens** can be transmitted through blood transfusions. These may include:
	1. Human Immunodeficiency Virus, whose rates increased by 500% in 2025.
	2. Hepatitis B, C, and D
7. **Electrolyte imbalances**:
	- Hyperkalemia can occur from hemolyzed blood cells releasing intracellular potassium.
	- Hypocalcemia can occur as citrate added to blood products bind ionized calcium.
	- Hypomagnesemia can also occur due to citrate binding.
8. **Transfusion-Associated Graph Versus Host Disease** (TA-GVHD) occurs when the donor lymphocytes attack recipient tissue—skin, liver, bone marrow. While rare, this can occur in immunocompromised patients. This process occurs as a delayed reaction, occurring 1 to 2 weeks after transfusion.

>[!SUCCESS] General Management of Adverse Transfusion Reactions
>Sequentially
>1. Stop the transfusion.
>2. Position patient upright and give oxygen.
>3. Change the IV tubing. Flush with normal saline at a KVO rate.
>4. Notify the health care provider
>5. Return the blood product to the laboratory for analysis.

## Potassium Imbalances
Potassium is a major intracellular cation (positively-charged ion), with only 2% of the body's potassium being found in the blood. It plays a major role in cardiac contractility. **Aldosterone** is a salt-saving hormone that manages the excretion of sodium in urine. When sodium is retained, potassium is excreted and vice versa.

>[!WARNING] Potassium-rich Food (mn. CROWDS)
>1. Carrots, cantaloupes
>2. Red meat (beef, pork) raisins
>3. Orange, organ meat
>4. Watermelon
>5. Dried fruits, dates, dark green leafy vegetables, dairy
>6. *Saging* (Banana), spinach, salt substitute
>
>Other sources include avocadoes, tomatoes, and potatoes.

>[!INFO] Potassium-poor Food (mn. BERRIES)
>This diet is relevant for patients who have cases of hyperkalemia or are at risk for the development of hyperkalemia.
>1. Berries
>2. Eggs
>3. Red apple (any apples in general)
>4. Rice
>5. Iceberg lettuce (light-colored vegetable, like cauliflowers)
>6. Eggplant
>7. Squash, shrimp
>
>White meats (chicken, fish) are also low in potassium.

### Hypokalemia
A serum potassium concentration of less than 3.5 mEq/L.

>[!WARNING] Causes of Hypokalemia (mn. DITCH)
>1. **Drugs** such as potassium-wasting diuretics (loop diuretics, thiazides), laxatives, [^27]insulin, sodium bicarbonate, corticosteroids therapy or medication that increase aldosterone or its effects.
>2. **Intermediate phase** of a burn injury. Hypokalemia may occur after the acute phase from fluid shifts and inadequate potassium replacement.
>3. ***Taas*** (elevated) **pH**, i.e., alkalosis. The body attempts to increase serum potassium concentrations by exchanging intracellular potassium with intravascular hydrogen ions (which dictate pH). A decrease in hydrogen ions raises pH—alkalosis.
>4. **Cushing's disease** is characterized by an overproduction of steroid hormones, one of which is aldosterone. Aldosterone retains sodium by excreting potassium in exchange.
>5. **Heavy fluid losses**, such as in vomiting, diarrhea, diuresis, and diaphoresis, is the most critical cause of hypokalemia. As only 2% of the body's potassium is in the intravascular space, profound effects can occur due to hypovolemia.
>
>Post-operatively, hypokalemia is also a risk if the stressors on the body result in the production of excess cortisol and steroids, which includes aldosterone.

Clinical manifestations of hypokalemia generally show a downwards trend, with the exception of urine output:
1. Increased urine output
2. Lethargy: anxiety, confusion, coma
3. Bradypnea: shallow, ineffective, diminished breath sounds from impaired neuromuscular excitability and muscle contraction.
4. Hypoactive and hypomotile gastrointestinal tract; constipation
5. Muscle weakness: leg cramps, deep tendon hyporeflexia, paresthesia
6. Thready, weak, irregular pulse; orthostatic hypotension; dysrhythmias
7. Electrocardiogram changes: ST depression; shallow, flat, or inverted T waves; and prominent U waves

**Correction of hypokalemia** can be done via:
1. **Dietary therapy** (refer to earlier info box)
2. **Change diuretics** to potassium-sparing diuretics. This includes (mn. *SEAT*) spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride, and triamterene. However, these can result in overcompensation—hyperkalemia.
3. **Potassium-containing solutions** such as potassium chloride (KCl) can be used for severe cases.
	- Potassium chloride oral solution or elixir has a very unpleasant, salty-metallic taste. It may be mixed with juice and other flavored non-carbonated drinks. It may not be mixed with grapefruit juice, carbonated beverages, and milk.
	- Potassium chloride is *only* given intravenously. When administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously, the highly irritating drug causes severe injury, pain, and necrosis to the tissue. However, it is never given as an IV push or as a bolus—this is immediately fatal. It is diluted (only up to 40 mEq/L) in IV fluids (frequently normal saline) before administration via infusion pump. Infusion rate is often limited to 5–10 mEq/hr in peripheral lines and 10–20 mEq/hr in central lines. Cardiac monitoring is done for central infusion or in patients at risk. Renal function is assessed as impaired renal function results in potassium retention.

### Hyperkalemia
A serum potassium concentration of more than 5.0 mEq/L

>[!WARNING] Causes of Hyperkalemia
>1. **Excess intake of potassium** from dietary sources or from infusion of potassium-containing solutions.
>2. **Inadequate excretion of potassium** from kidney disease, inadequate aldosterone (e.g., Addison's), and potassium-sparing diuretics.
>3. **Intracellular to extracellular shift of potassium**, such as in cases of burns and massive cell destruction.
>
>(mn. *MACHINE*):
>- Medications: potassium-sparing diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium chloride
>- Acidosis: the body attempts to compensate by shifting potassium out of cells in exchange for moving hydrogen ions out of the vascular compartment.
>- Cell lysis conditions: tumor lysis syndrome, crush injuries, sickle cell disease, or hemolysis release intracellular potassium into the blood, causing hyperkalemia.
>- Hypoaldosteronism (Addison's disease) increases sodium excretion, resulting in reduced potassium excretion
>- Intake of excessive potassium-rich food (enumerated earlier).
>- Nephron damage, as this results in an increase in many electrolytes—sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium, phosphate. Only calcium is decreased when reduced kidney function exists.
>- Emergent phase of burns; the first, acute phase. Cell lysis results in an excessive release of potassium into the blood.

Clinical manifestations of hyperkalemia generally show an upwards trend, with the exception of urine output.
1. Decreased urine output
2. Anxiety
3. Bradypnea: profound muscle weakness (impaired neuromuscular transmission) results in respiratory failure.
4. Hyperactive and hypermotile gastrointestinal tract; diarrhea.
5. Muscle twitches, cramping, paresthesias, then later profound weakness, reaching the trunk, head, and respiratory muscles when lethal levels are reached.
6. Slow, weak, irregular heart rate
7. Electrocardiogram changes: tall peaked T waves, flat P waves, widened QRS complexes, and prolonged PR intervals.

>[!DRUG] Management for Potassium Chloride
>1. Stop the procedure
>2. Stabilize the patient’s heart by giving calcium gluconate or calcium chloride—an electrolyte that counteracts the effects of potassium.
>3. Induce shifting of extracellular potassium into cells:
>		- Insulin with D50 (to prevent hypoglycemia).
>		- Sodium bicarbonate
>4. Eliminate excess potassium:
>		- Potassium-wasting diuretics: loop diuretics, thiazides
>		- Kayexelate, a laxative, which promotes excretion of potassium through the gastrointestinal tract.
>		- Sodium polystyrene sulfonate (SPS), or more recently sodium zirconium cyclosilicate, are binders that bind with potassium in the gastrointestinal tract that reduces the free potassium concentration in the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract, thereby lowering serum potassium levels.

Along with the interventions above, **hyperkalemia** can also be managed by:
1. Avoiding high-potassium food; consuming low-potassium food.
2. Avoiding hidden potassium sources, such as salt substitutes.

## Sodium
Sodium is normally present in the serum at concentrations of 135 to 145 mEq/L. Sodium is correlated with fluid balance and is directly proportional to **aldosterone** activity.

>[!EXAMPLE] Sources of Sodium
>The most common source of sodium is **table salt**. Many sources of salt are present in processed foods, junk foods, smoked and cured food, and pickled food.

### Hyponatremia
A sodium level less than 135 mEq/L.

>[!WARNING] Causes of Hyponatremia (mn. NO SALT)
>1. **Na intake is low**, such as in clients partaking in low-salt diets (e.g., DASH diet, bland diet)
>2. **Overload of fluids** dilute the body's supply of sodium, resulting in dilutional hyponatremia. This can occur in conditions such as congestive heart failure, water intoxication, [^28]oxytocin toxicity, overinfusion, etc.
>3. **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone** (SIADH) also results in dilution hyponatremia.
>4. **Addison's Disease**
>5. **Loss of Sodium** from sources such as diaphoresis, and [^29]diuretics.
>6. **Too much infusion of hypotonic solutions**

Clinical manifestations depend on whether sodium was actually lost in the body, i.e., "true sodium loss".
1. If **sodium is lost**, water is also lost, which results in signs of shock: flat veins, thready pulses, poor skin turgor, delayed capillary refill, etc.
2. If **water gain** is responsible for hyponatremia (dilutional hyponatremia), signs of fluid overload are present: distended veins, bounding pulses, edema, etc.

**Complications of low sodium** is most severe when affecting the brain. By virtue of osmosis, hyponatremia will result in **cerebral edema** as the (relatively) sodium-rich cells pull water from the sodium-poor blood.
- This increases intracranial pressure and produces **Cushing's triad**: hypertension, bradycardia, and bradypnea (opposite of the usual manifestations of shock). It also produces **widened pulse pressure** (>40 mm Hg), **bounding pulses**, **hyperthermia** (>40°⁠C), **dilated pupils** (a.k.a. papilledema),
- To control intracranial pressure, interventions include:
	- **Elevate the head of bed by 30°** but not more than 45°.
	- **Decrease environmental stimuli** (dim lights, lessen noise, limit visitors, [^30]avoid valsalva maneuver)
	- **Osmotic diuresis** with mannitol.
	- **Infuse hypertonic saline solutions** to reverse the effects of hyponatremia. Gradual correction is performed as the use of highly concentrated solutions (e.g., 5% saline) results in rapid and excessive correction of hyponatremia, which dehydrates the brain cells in a complication known as [^31]*osmotic demyelination syndrome* (ODS). Solutions like 3% saline may produce better client outcomes.
- **Seizure precautions** are put in place for clients with increased intracranial pressure.
	1. The nursing priority is maintaining the **airway**:
		- Removing dentures when not eating, retainers, etc.
		- Prepare an endotracheal tube, suction machine, and tracheostomy set in case it becomes necessary. Padded tongue blades and other equipment that is placed in the mouth during seizures are not used; [^32]nothing should be placed in the mouth during a seizure.
	2. Secondarily, establish **breathing**: oxygen therapy with a bag-valve mask. Circulation is not a concern for patients with a seizure.
	3. Next, **ensure safety**.
		- Modify the environment to reduce both vulnerability and hazards: set the bed to its lowest height, raise the side rails, and apply [^33]padding to the side rails.
		- When a seizure occurs outside of bed, the patient is lowered to the floor (even when they are in a chair).
		- No body part of the patient is restrained due to the risk for injury. Protect the head from banging against the floor by providing a cushion, but do not hold the head in place.

**Correction of hyponatremia** can be done via:
1. Increased salt intake
2. Infusion of saline solution
3. Treatment of SIADH via fluid restriction and ADH antagonists (Tolvaptan, Demeclocycline).

### Hypernatremia
A serum sodium level greater than 145 mEq/L.

>[!WARNING] Causes of Hypernatremia
>1. **Increased sodium intake**
>2. **Sodium retention** such as in cases of chronic kidney disease and cushing's disease.
>3. **Hemoconcentration**, which reduces serum fluid content while retaining electrolytes (e.g., [^34]diabetes insipidus).

Clinical manifestations of hypernatremia also depend on the present etiologies:
1. Increased sodium content results in **hypertension**.
2. Hemoconcentration hypernatremia, such as in diabetes insipidus, may feature **hypotension**. Hemoconcentration also triggers the thirst mechanism.

**Complications of low sodium** is most severe when affecting the brain. By virtue of osmosis, hypernatremia will result in **cerebral dehydration** as the sodium-rich blood pull water from the (relatively) sodium-poor brain. This also results in a potential for **seizures**, and therefore management is the same as discussed in [[#Hyponatremia]].

**Correction of hypernatremia** can be done via:
1. **Decreased dietary sodium intake**, which can be helped with the use of salt substitutes and herbs and spices to replace dietary salt.
2. **Infusion of non-saline hypotonic solutions**, most commonly [^35]D5W.
3. **Diabetes insipidus** is corrected with fluid replacement and medications (Desmopressin \[DDAVP]).

## Calcium
Calcium is normally present in the blood at concentrations of 8.5 to 10.5 mEq/L. Calcium is important for *Cs*: clotting, cardiac contractility, and calming nerves and cells. It is also a mineral important for enhancing bone and teeth strength.

>[!TIP] Regulators of Calcium Balance
>1. **Calcitonin**, produced by the thyroid, has a hypocalcemic effect by stimulating osteoblastic activity and calcium deposition (reabsorption, mineralization) in bones.
>2. **Parathyroid hormone** (Parathormone, PTH), produced by the parathyroids, has a hypercalcemic effect by stimulating osteoclastic activity and calcium resorption (demineralization) from the bones.
>3. **Calcitriol** is the [^36]activated form of Vitamin D, which enhances calcium absorption.

### Hypocalcemia
A serum sodium level of less than 9 mg/dL.

>[!WARNING] Causes of Hypocalcemia (mn. CHAMP)
>1. **Calcium intake is low**
>2. **Hypoparathyroidism**, as PTH results in bone demineralization.
>3. **Hyperphosphatemia**, as calcium and phosphate have an inverse relationship, i.e., high phosphorus results in low calcium.
>4. **Alcoholism**
>5. **Malabsorption disorders**, especially [^37]Celiac's disease (gluten intolerance) and Crohn's disease.
>6. [^38]**Pancreatitis** results in a backflow of lipase that results in the autodigestion of adipose, which produces free fatty acids (FFA), which then binds with calcium to form calcium soaps. This reduces the available calcium electrolytes in the body. This process, resulting in calcium soap formation, is known as **saponification**.

Clinical manifestations of hypocalcemia relates to its functions:
1. *"Calming nerves and cells"*: a reduction in calcium results in **tetany**, which **appears** in many muscles but significantly in [^39]**laryngospasm**, **clonus**, and **seizures**.
	- Prepare an endotracheal tube, suctioning machine, and tracheostomy set.
	- **Chvostek's sign** is a sign of irritability of the facial nerve (CN VII) that results in **facial twitching** and blinking due to hypocalcemia.
	- **Trosseau's sign** is also a sign of irritability elicited by inflating a blood pressure cuff around the upper arm for 3 to 5 minutes, which should result in a **carpopedal spasm** (inward flexion, adducted thumb).
	- **Clonus** (+4 hyperreflexia) can appear in severe hypotension.
2. "*Cardiac contractility*": a reduction in calcium can result in **cardiac dysrhythmias**.
3. "*Clotting*": a reduction in calcium results in easy bleeding.
	- Advise the patient to use soft-bristled toothbrushes, electric razors, and avoid activities that can result in injury.
	- Observe for **signs of bleeding**: gingival bleeding, petechiae, hematoma.

**Correction of hypocalcemia** can be done via:
1. **Increased dietary calcium intake**
2. **Infusion of Plain Lactate Ringer's**, which contains
3. **Administration of calcium gluconate**.

### Hypercalcemia
A serum calcium concentration of greater than 11 mg/dL (5.5 mEq/L).

>[!WARNING] Causes of Hypercalcemia (mn. HIGH CAL)
>1. **Hyperparathyroidism**, as PTH results in bone demineralization.
>2. **Immobility**, which promotes the breakdown of bone mineral.
>3. **Glucocorticoids**, e.g., Prednisone
>4. **Hypophosphatemia**, as calcium and phosphate have an inverse relationship, i.e., low phosphorus results in high calcium.
>5. **Calcium containing medications**
>6. **Antacids**, especially calcium carbonate ("Tums")
>7. **maLignancy** (cancer) most commonly metastasizes to the bone along with the brain and lungs. The presence of cancer cells in the bone may result in bone breakdown. Alternatively, malignancies can also secrete substances that mimic the effects of PTH.

Clinical manifestations of hypercalcemia relates to its functions:
1. "*Calming nerves and cells*": an elevation in calcium results in excessive **relaxation**, which appears in many muscles but significantly in **respiratory failure**.
2. "*Clotting*": an elevation in calcium results in excessive clotting. This may result in an ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, and other occlusive diseases.
3. **Weakened skeletal integrity** appears if hypercalcemia is a result of bone demineralization. Fractures may easily occur, and pathologic fractures may appear in severe cases.
	- **Promote safety, especially from falls**. Environmental modifications are very useful, such as removing rugs and [^40]painting stairs to increase contrast.
4. **Renal calculi** can form as the kidneys attempt to filter calcium from the blood. Increase fluid intake to reduce the risk of calculus formation.

**Correction of hypercalcemia** can be done via:
1. **Increase fluid intake** to dilute calcium.
2. **Infusion of plain saline solution** to dilute calcium.
3. **Administration of loop diuretics** (furosemide) to expel calcium in the urine.
4. **Administer calcitonin** to promote calcium deposition into bone to reduce serum concentration.

>[!INFO] Similarities with Magnesium
>Magnesium is also a muscle relaxant. As such, hypomagnesemia can also result in Chvostek's and Trosseau's signs, hyperreflexia and tonicity, etc. Inversely, hypermagnesemia can result in complications such as respiratory failure from excessive muscle relaxation.

# Oxygenation
## Assessment of the Respiratory System
The nurse begins assessment of the respiratory system via the IPPA framework, beginning with inspection, then palpation, percussion, and auscultation.
1. **Inspection**:
	- **Skin color** may become [^41]cyanotic in late hypoxia and cherry-red in late carbon monoxide poisoning.
	- **Clubbing of the fingers** that appears due to chronic hypoxia.
		- This is checked with Schamroth's (window) test, which is positive when there is no diamond-shaped window when two nails of the same finger of both hands are placed together back-to-back.
		- This can also be quantified by Lavibond's angle--the angle of the base of the nail relative to the finger--which is normally less than 180° (~160°). An angle of 180° (the base of the nail is in line with the finger) indicates mild hypoxia. An angle greater than 180° is found in clubbing.
	- Observe for the **use of accessory muscles** for respiration.
	- Observe for **paradoxical breathing**, chest excursion during inhalation and chest incursion during exhalation. This is common in flail chest, a type of multiple rib fracture.
	- Observe for **retractions**, a visible depression of the skin due to increased respiratory effort. This is more commonly seen in children.
	- Determine the **anteroposterior-to-lateral (APL) diameter ratio** (normally 1:2) and **anteroposterior-to-transverse diameter ratio**.
		- A ratio of 1:1, referred to as a barrel chest or drum chest, is found in **emphysema**, a type of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
		- A ratio of 2:1 can be found in *pectus carinatum*, also known as Pigeon chest. One case in which this may appear in individuals includes patients with loose connective tissues (Marfan's syndrome).
		- A ratio of as severe as 1:5, found in *pectus excavatum*, may also be found in Marfan's syndrome.
	- Determine the patient's **breathing pattern**. The nurse observes for the breathing rate, rhythm, and depth.
		1. **Eupnea**: normal rate, rhythm and depth. The normal respiratory rate in adults is 12 to 20 cpm.
		2. **Bradypnea**: slow rate, and normal rhythm and depth.
		3. **Tachypnea**: fast rate, and normal rhythm and depth.
		4. **Hyperpnea**: normal rate and rhythm, and deep depth. This is normal after exercise and those who live in high altitude areas.
		5. **Kussmaul's Breathing** (Hyperventilation): fast and deep breathing with normal rhythm that may appear due to anxiety and stress or as a [^42]compensatory mechanism for metabolic acidosis. The visualization of this breathing is dubbed as a "sawtooth pattern".
		6. **Apnea** is the cessation of breathing.
			- This may be normal in infants as long as it lasts for less than 10 seconds and no signs of respiratory distress (nasal flaring, cyanosis, retractions).
		7. **Cheyne-Stokes Breathing** is a "*waxing and waning*" pattern with periods of apnea. This may be observed in an [^43]acute increase in intracranial pressure due to dysfunction in the respiratory center. It can also be seen in congestive heart failure.
		8. **Biot's Breathing** is a breathing pattern that has no standard rate, rhythm, or depth. It is also known as ataxia or cluster breathing.
			- This can be observed in clients experiencing an [^44]opioid overdose.
			- It can also be seen in chronic increased intracranial pressure such as hydrocephaly. It can be a sequelae of cheyne-stokes breathing.
			- It can also be seen in patients with brain stem injury due to damage done to the respiratory center.
2. **Palpation**:
	- Assess for **chest excursion**/**chest expansion** by placing the thumbs on the level of T9-T10. Then, the patient is instructed to inhale deeply. This gives the nurse a feel for how much each lung expanded; the expected finding is symmetrical expansion of both lungs.
		- If unequal, this may hint at the presence of atelectasis or restrictive disorders that prevent lung expansion (e.g., hemothorax).
	- Assess for [^45]**tactile fremitus** by placing the palms (or fingertips, finger pads, lower palm, or ulnar aspect of the hand) on the patient's back then asking the patient to repeat a phase ("One, two, three", "E, E, E", "Blue moon", "Ninety-nine"). This should elicit fremitus, vibration produced by sound. The expected finding is a symmetric, normal intensity fremitus.
		- The intensity of fremitus may be increased by *consolidation*, which is a state where the normally air-filled spaces are replaced with solids or fluids. This enhances the transmission of vibrations through tissues, which makes fremitus stronger.
		- The intensity of fremitus may be decreased by *air trapping*, which reduces the transmission of vibrations through tissues, which makes fremitus weaker. Fremitus becomes absent in atelectasis.
3. **Percussion** elicits sound from the lungs by controlled strikes to the various lung fields. The only normal finding is a **resonant** percussion sound, a hollow sound.
	- [^46]**Flat** percussion sound: extreme dullness, often brought about by [^47]**pleural effusion**.
	- [^48]**Dull** percussion sound: dull, thud-like sound, often brought about in **consolidation**.
	- **Hyperresonance** percussion sound: a booming sound brought about by **air trapping**.
	- [^49]**Tympany** percussion sound: a drum-like sound brought about by the presence of a **tension pneumothorax**.
4. **Auscultation**:
	- Normal breath sounds can be heard as bronchial, bronchovesicular, and vesicular breath sounds in their appropriate places. These become [^50]adventitious (abnormal) when pitches do not match their expected positions.
		- **Bronchial/Tracheal**: a normal, high-pitched, coarse breath sound heard at the level of the trachea, only heard anteriorly.
		- **Bronchovesicular**: a normal, medium-pitched breath sound heard at the level of the scapula over the bronchi, at the 1st to 2nd intercostal space along the sternal borders.
		- **Vesicular**: a normal, low-pitched breath sound heard at the lung fields. This is described as a "*sighing*" sound.
	- Adventitious breath sounds
		- **Crackles**, also known as **rales**, are popping, gurgling, crackling sounds produced by the movement of air through mucus and fluids. It is heard better during inspiration. Depending on the location of secretions, the breath sound appears as:
			- **Coarse**: passing of air through secretions in the bronchi, sounds like velcro ripping apart.
			- **Fine**: passing of air through secretions in the alveoli, sounds like hair rubbing together.
		- **Wheezing** is a musical breath sound appearing during expiration caused by bronchoconstriction and therefore most commonly observed in asthma, and can also be heard in mild anaphylaxis. This may be observed as:
			- **Sonorous/Rhonchi wheezing**: "*snore-like*", "*groaning*", or "*moaning*" low-pitched sound.
			- **Sibilant wheezing**: high-pitched. This is a more worrying manifestation as narrower airways often result in higher pitched wheezing.
		- **Friction Rub** is a grating, leather-like rubbing sound that appears as two mucosae rub against each other. This may be caused by pleuritis/pleurisy (pleural friction rub) or pericarditis ([^51]pericardial friction rub). This is heard consistently, but best heard during inhalation at maximal expansion.
			- A friction rub can be better heard when the patient is positioned leaning forward or in a lateral recumbent position, which shifts the mediastinal organs forward, allowing it to be heard better.
		- **Stridor** is the most concerning breath sound, described as a harsh, violent, croaking, crowing, high-pitched, hoarse, whistling sound. It is caused by **obstruction**, e.g., choking, anaphylaxis, laryngitis.
			- Stridor is heard during **inspiration** if the obstruction is in the upper airway (mouth to larynx) and during **expiration** if the obstruction is in the lower airway (trachea to alveoli).

## Diagnostic Examination of the Lungs
1. **Imaging studies**:
	- X-ray, CT scan, MRI scan are often only screening tests.
		- X-rays are contraindicated in pregnancy.
		- Brassieres and all jewelry are removed in all these tests.
		- MRI scans require the removal of all [^52]metals on the body, and is contraindicated in patients with pacemakers, prosthesis, stents, non-biologic heart valve replacements, etc.
	- CT scan with Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA) is confirmatory for [^53]pulmonary embolism. The dye used is commonly iodinated, which makes it a priority for the nurse to implement safety protocols:
		1. Inquire about **allergies to seafood, specifically shellfish**. This provides insight into a potential allergy to iodine.
		2. Assess **bleeding risk**. The dye is injected invasively, which makes bleeding a concern. Ask the patient about the use of [^54]bleeding medications.
		3. Check **renal status**. The best indicator for renal function is the glomerular filtration rate. Also check BUN and Creatinine. This is important because contrast can result in contrast-induced nephropathy.
		4. Assess if the patient is taking [^55]**metformin**. Metformin may react with contrast to form lactic acid, which results in acidosis. Metformin should be held for 24 or 48 hours prior? to the procedure.
		5. Assess for **pregnancy status**. Potential teratogenic effects may occur.
2. **Ventilation-Perfusion (V/Q) Scan** is an alternative to CTPA for the diagnosis of a pulmonary embolism. This is a test that determines the ratio between ventilation (airflow) and perfusion (blood flow). Instead of iodinated contrast, radioactive (xenon or technetium) contrast is injected for perfusion and inhaled for ventilation.
	- Imaging of the radioactive contrast is done for each of the inhaled and injected radiation, then compared. A difference between blood flow and airflow is called a **V/Q mismatch**.
## Interventions for the Respiratory System
1. **Incentive Spirometers** are an exercise device for the lungs to help the lungs expand.
	- Commonly used by post-operative patients, especially for general anesthesia and major operations.
	- The patient sits in an upright position, places the tip of the mouthpiece just past the tip of the lips, then inhales slowly and gently.
	- Once maximal inhalation has been reached, the patient holds their breath for three to five seconds.
	- Incentive spirometry is repeated from 10 to 15 times per hour.
2. **Peak Expiratory Flow Meter** (PEFM) is used to diagnose the severity of the manifestations of asthma. It is a small handheld device with indicators for determining the flow rate the patient is able to exhale into the device.
	- The patient is instructed to blow hard and fast into the mouthpiece of the device.
	- Repeat for three times, recording the best performance from the three results.
	- A result in the **green range** (80% to 100%) is only mild, which is good. The nurse only needs to continue monitoring the patient during episodes.
	- A result in the **yellow range** (50% to 80%) is moderate. A rescue inhaler is required to deliver short-acting asthma medications.
	- A result in the **red range** (<50%) is severe. Medications and hospitalization is required.
3. **Oxygen therapy**: the delivery of oxygen in concentrations greater than atmospheric oxygen (~21%) to enhance respiration. This can be a nursing action from flow rates of 1 to 3 LPM, but is a dependent nursing action for flow rates 4 LPM or greater.
	- Beginning from the oxygen source, a flow meter is attached, followed by the humidifier, then the tubing.
	- **Fire Precautions**:
		- Attach a no-smoking sign in a highly visible area, e.g., the door, the bed, the tank.
		- Avoid any sources of fire (e.g., gas appliances, curling iorns) and prepare a fire extinguisher.
		- Ensure the patient is able to ear fire alarms (e.g., ensure functioning hearing aids)
		- Do not apply nail polish or color; the nail beds are valuable indicators of oxygen status.
		- Do not use woolen or synthetic fabrics that can create static charge and ignite fire. Cotton fabrics should be used instead.
		- If the patient catches on fire, remember "Stop, Drop, and Roll".
		- If a building catches fire, remember "RACE: Rescue, Alarm, Contain, Extinguish/Evacuate"
			- Rescue ambulatory patients first
			- Pull the fire alarm
			- Close the doors and windows to contain the fire
			- Extinguish and evacuate
	- Oxygen tanks must be stored vertically.
	- Flow rate: the patient is instructed not to adjust or alter the flow rate.
4. **Oxygen delivery devices**:
	- Venturi device: most precise, commonly used for COPD patients to maintain their hypoxic drive.
	- Non-rebreather mask: highest flow (100%) of oxygen by removing all exhaled gas and replacing it with pure oxygen, used for severe hypoxia.
	- The nurse's priority is to keep the bag inflated at all times. It must be inflated prior to application on the client.
	- A non-rebreather mask can result in death as the oxygen tank is the client's only source of oxygen. Constant monitoring is required.
	- An oxygen tent can be used for infants who are likely to remove nasal cannulae and face masks.
	- A face tent can be used for patients who have contraindications such as facial burns.
5. An **Endotracheal Tube** (ET tube) are passed through the oral or nasal cavities into the trachea for oxygen delivery.
	- The oral route is more common and preferred. It is also easier to insert through the oral route.
	- The nasal route is used if an oral obstruction or contraindication is present.
	- An endotracheal tube is usually 6 to 9 mm in diameter.
	- Endotracheal tube insertion is done for a maximum of 30 seconds by a physician.
	- The nurse assists with oxygenation via bag-valve mask.
	- After insertion, the priority assessment is to confirm tube placement, most reliably via x-ray. Secondarily, capnometers may be used to determine the presence of carbon dioxide, which is a byproduct of the lungs and therefore should be present (35 to 45 mm Hg). Auscultation can also be done.
6. A **Tracheostomy** is the creation of a stoma over the trachea for long-term ventilation. This is done for patients with upper airway obstruction.
	1. **Outer cannula**: the cannula held in place by a balloon to secure it in place. It keeps the airway open.
	2. **Inner cannula**: collects secretions, which is why
		- Cleans with half-strength hydrogen peroxide solution to liquefy thick secretions.
		- Rinse with PNSS.
	3. **Obturator**: guides re-insertion of the inner cannula at all times. This is taped at the head of the bed (or at the bedside).
	4. **Plug**: placed on the outer cannula once the patient is weaning off of the tracheostomy during recovery.
	5. **Cuff**: Holds the outer cannula in place. Inflated to 14 to 20 mm Hg to avoid dislodgement and tracheal necrosis
		- Assessment of the balloon pressure via a *manometer* is done at least once a shift.
		- Always prepare a smaller (or equal) tracheostomy tube at the bedside in case of emergency.
		- In the event of **dislodgement**:
			1. Reinsert the inner cannula using an obturator.
			2. If insertion is difficult or an obturator is not present, a curved hemostate (e.g., curved Kelly forceps) can be inserted through the stoma then opened to establish an airway.
			3. Cover the stoma with occlusive (non-porous) dressing, and facilitate breathing via a bag-valve mask.
		- In the event of obstruction by secretions:
			1. Pre-oxygenate the patient via a bag-valve mask of 100% oxygen for 30 seconds to 3 minutes.
			2. Insert the suction catheter without suction until resistance is met. Once resistance is met, withdraw by 1 to 2 centimeters.
			3. Initiate intermittent suctioning while withdrawing in a circular motion for at most 10 seconds.
			4. Rinse the catheter with plain saline.
		- In tying knots:
			1. Tie-tie-untie
			2. Square knot
7. **Chest Thoracostomy**: an incision made on the thorax to drain air or fluid from the thoracic cavity, usually the pleural cavity (i.e., pleural effusion). A **chest tube drainage system** allows for the drainage of this excess effusion to re-expand the lungs.
	- The chest tube drainage system is composed of three bottles:
		 1. The first bottle serves as the collection/drainage bottle, and is the only bottle not to contain water. It is where drained effusions stay.
			- Drainage should be less than 100 mL/hr. Any more is always pathogenic, and can result in shock.
			- Assessment of the amount of drainage is done every hour, and can be done by a nursing aide.
			- Documentation is done [^56]incrementally.
			- Never allow the drainage to reach the tubing. This will result in a negation of the suction. The bottle is emptied as needed.
		2. The second bottle serves as a water seal. This is where air from the pleura is trapped.
			- There should always be at least 2 centimeters of sterile water in the water seal bottle.
			- There should be gentle intermittent bubbling.
			- Continuous bubbling is initially expected after insertion and when the patient is coughing and sneezing.
			 - There should be tidaling (fluctuation, oscillation) of the water level. This occurs in sync with the patient's breathing. The absence of tidaling can be an indicator of lung re-expansion or obstruction. In case of obstruction, instruct the patient to cough. If ineffective, inform the physician.
		3. The third bottle serves as the suction. This facilitates the removal of effused substances. An air vent is present to remove excess pressure.
			- A pressure of -10 to -20 mm Hg is usually used.
			- There is at least 20 cm of water in the suction bottle.
			- There should be gentle continuous bubbling in the suction bottle. Vigorous bubbling is indicative of excessive pressure. Absent bubbling may indicate malfunctioning of the suction machine.
			- There should be no tidaling in the suction bottle.
		- Contraindicated actions and abnormalities:
			- Do not milk or strip the tubing as this may result in lung tissue trauma.
			- Do not clamp the tubing, as this may result in tension pneumothorax. The only time clamping is permitted is a brief clamp to attempt to localize the location of an air leak (whether it is the patient or the system).
			- Vigorous, continuous bubbling is a sign of an air leak. This may occur in cases of tubing disconnecting from the system (reconnect the tube), if bottles become cracked (submerge tip at least 1 inch in a bottle of sterile water while replacing bottle), or dislodgement (cover the thoracostomy with an occlusive non-porous dressing taped on three sides).
			- In the absence of bubbling and fluctuation, an obstruction or lung re-expansion is suspected. Observe the patient for signs of distress, which supports the presence of an obstruction.
