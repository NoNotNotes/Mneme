# Community Organizing Participatory Action Research
**COPAR** is done for the sake of community development. It is done in collaboration with the community. It aims to transform passive communities to active communities. Its goals include (mn. *SAME*):
1. **Self-reliance**, the main and most important goal.
2. **Active community participation**
3. **Mobilization**
4. **Empowerment**

These goals may be achieved through the following methodologies:
1. **Action-Reflection-Action Sessions**, similar to the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) method in leadership and management, follows an implementation-evaluation-reimplementation framework to improve community actions.
2. **Consciousness Raising** is a method to increase the awareness of the community towards deficits in the community, shifting their mindset from that of a "victim" to that of responsibility.
3. **Mass-based Participation** attempts to implement programs for the people and with the people. It does not limit itself to the individual but rather attempts to target the entire community.
4. **Group-Centered** involves the creation of organizations and a group of community leaders that, along with collective leadership, helps sustain community programs and development even after the phase-out stage of community organization.

## Phases of COPAR (Maglaya)
(mn. *POEIT*)
1. **Preparatory Phase**
	- Preparation begins with **area/site selection**. The target of community development ideally fits a profile (mn. *DEPRESSED*):
		- Depressed (low standard of living), exploited, poor
		- Residential (100-200 families)
		- Economically depressed
		- Safe (main priority)
		- Shows high mortality and morbidity cases
		- Evaluate existing health programs to determine what health programs are necessary
		- Do not have health facilities, as communities are often far-flung.
	- Once a site has been selected, an **ocular inspection** via [^1]windshield (a.k.a., rapid) survey. The creation of a spot map may be done during this phase.
	- In preparation for immersion in the community, the **identification of a foster family** is done to act as hosts for the nurse/s' stay in the community.
	- **Community profiling**/**social investigation** is the formation of baseline data for the community as an initial assessment. This involves demographics, health status, existing services (health programs, school programs, non-government organization programs), the physical environment, and power structures within the community (leaders and other influential individuals/groups).
	- **Development of survey tools** should be completed prior to immersion, as the community setting may not be conducive to the creation of survey tools. These are made based on the data obtained from community profiling.
	- Afterwards, **entry and integration** may begin. In this step, the nurse enters the community, generally following five steps (mn. RISER):
		- **Rapport establishment**. At the same time, contract-setting is done to outline the nurse's stay.
		- **Imbibe with the way of life** in the community.
		- **Share Experiences**.
		- **Respect the community**.
2. **Organizational Phase**:
	- **Social preparation**, the persuasion of the community and its leaders to collaborate with the nurse and identification of leaders. The criteria for the selection of potential leaders includes (mn. *TORPE*): time (has extra time), oriented to change/open-minded, respected by the community, poor (from the poorest sectors for representation), and effective communication skills.
	- **Election of officers** occur once nominees have been chosen with the previous step. This involves the *creation of an organization*, which formalizes their position. This organization will be responsible for maintaining and developing health programs after the nurse leaves the community.
	- **Planning of organizational activities** begins once an organization has been created.
3. **Education and Training Phase**:
	- The most important activity of this phase is the **creation of community diagnoses**.
	- This also includes the actual **implementation** of organizational activities through **mobilization** of the community.
	- The nurse partake in health services including **health education** and **health promotion and disease prevention**.
	- **Evaluation of existing health services** can also help identify potential improvements and changes to be made in their implementation for mobilization.
	- **Training of community health workers and leaders**.
4. **Intersectoral Collaboration Phase**: the nurse collaborates with other sectors for additional funding, intersectoral implementation, and sustainable
5. **Termination Phase**/**Phase-out Phase**: the nurse/s end their time with the community with the **transferring of responsibility to the community**. The nurse ensures independence and self-reliance before phasing out, and may provide further consultation with the community afterwards.
## Principles of COPAR
(mn. *4Ps Dadami Camote si Sara*)
1. **People-centered**
2. **Participative** in nature, with a target of self-reliance.
3. **Process-oriented**, following a framework of steps
4. **Poorest sectors** are the target of COPAR.
5. **Democratic**
6. **Developmental**
7. **Change-oriented**
8. **Self-reliance** is the main goal of COPAR

## Community Health Nursing Process
The community health nursing process follows the clinical nursing process (ADPIE) but merges the steps of assessment and diagnosis (APIE).
1. **Community Analysis**: rapport establishment, community profiling, then problem-identification (community diagnosis).
2. **Planning**: design and initiation with the community.
3. **Implementation**: mobilization of the community.
4. **Program maintenance and consolidation** (transferring of responsibility) for sustenance. This phase includes orientation of and endorsement to the community.
5. **Re-assessment** (evaluation) is necessary to determine what adjustments need to be made to sustain health programs. Communities and their health status are naturally dynamic, and change over time.

## Critical Steps in COPAR
1. **Integration**
2. **Community Profiling**/**Social Investigation**
3. **Program Planning**: identification of leaders and stakeholders
4. **Community Dialogues**, part of consciousness raising.
5. **Mobilization**
6. **Reflection**: community-led feedback for the programs.

## Training Programs
1. **Self-Awareness and Leadership Training** (SALT):
	- Topics pertaining to oneself and one's country.
	- A common method of developing self-awareness is Johari's window.
	- Dicussion of the healthcare delivery system, primary health care, universal health care, and the level of care/prevention (primary, secondary, tertiary).
	- Topics on the current Philippine health situation
	- Leadership styles
2. **Participatory Action Research and Management Training**
	- Basic research
	- Managerial skills (mn. PODC): planning, organizing, directing, controlling.
3. **Basic Health Skills Training**
	- Aseptic technique and proper handwashing
	- Bag technique
	- Vital signs taking: blood pressure technique
	- Isolation techniques: quarantine (suspected cases), isolation (confirmed cases)
4. **Advanced Health Skills Training** often includes topics related to disaster and emergency response:
	- Basic Life Support
	- First Aid Training
	- Triaging via the START protocol, marking patients as green (walking wounded), yellow (delayed), red (immediate, priority), black (expectant, dead), orange (radioactive hazard), or purple (biohazard).
5. **Specialized Health Skills Training**:
	- **Traditional and Alternative Medicine Act** (R.A. 8423): the use of alternative therapies and the approved 10 medicinal plants by the DOH. Refer to [[276a-traditional-medicine|Traditional Medicine]] for the full discussion of both herbal medicine and other alternative therapies.

# Primary Health Care
Essential Healthcare made universally accessible, available, appropriate, and affordable:
1. Accessible
2. Available
3. Appropriate
4. Affordable

>[!TIP] Mission: To strengthen the healthcare system by increasing opportunities and supporting conditions.

## Declarations
1. **Alma Ata Declaration**: a declaration in Alma Ata, USSR (now Almaty, Kazakhstan) on Sept. 6-12, 1978 that established the "Health For All By Year 2000". This was the first global conference on Primary Health Care (PHC).
	- The Philippines adopted PHC through **LOI 949** on October 19, 1979 signed by President Ferdinand Marcos, aiming for health in the hands of the people by 2020.
2. **Astana Declaration**: a declaration in Astana, Kazakhstan ib Oct. 25-26, 2018 that established Universa; Health Coverage and Sustainable Development Goals.
	- The Philippines adopted UHC through **R.A. 11223** on February 20, 2019 signed by President Rodrigo Duterte, aiming for "Health for all, no filipino must be left behind."

## Key Players of PHC
1. **Department of Health**: the main health department of the Philippines.
	- The central office of the DOH is in San Lazaro, Manila, within San Lazaro Hospital, the main hospital for communicable disease in the Philippines.
	- The **secretary of health**, currently Dr. Teodoro Herbosa in 2025, is the head of DOH.
2. **World Health Organization**: the main health department of the United States.
	- The central office of the WHO is in Geneva, Switzerland.
	- The Philippines WHO headquarters is in United Nations Avenue, Taft.
	- The Western Pacific Who headquarters is ib San Lazaro, Manila.
	- The **secretary-general**, Dr. Tedros Adhanrom Ghebreyesus, holds the highest position in the WHO.
	- The Philippines is included in the Western Pacific region.
3. **United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund** (UNICEF) is the implementing arm of the WHO, often funding the health programs.
	- The IMCI program is a program by WHO and UNICEF

## Pillars of Healthcare
1. **Active community participation**
2. **Intra- and intersectoral linkages**
3. **Use of appropriate technology**, referring to available resources. This is pertinent for indigent tehnologies.
4. **Support mechanisms made available**, referring to DOH programs.

## Elements of Primary Health Care
%% (mn. *I SEE MALe He Comm*) %%
1. **Immunization**
2. **Sanitation**, based on **P.D. 856**, the Code on Sanitation.
	- The WHO established the **Global WASH** program in response to major morbidity and mortality related to unclean water sources. This program outlines proper water sanitation and hygiene standards.
	- Food sanitation is managed by permits (from the municipal office, sanitary inspector, and signed by the chairman). A food certificate is also given to food handlers. There are four rights of food sanitation:
		1. **Right Source**
		2. **Right Preparation**
		3. **Right Cooking**
		4. **Right Storage**
	- Waste Management is based on **R.A. 9053**, the Ecological Waste Management Act. This also details the segregation of wastes according to color:
		1. **Green**: biodegradable or wet trash.
		2. **Yellow**: infectious trash
		3. **Black**: non-biodegradable or dry trash.

3. **Essential Drugs** need to be kept accessible in each community, especially far-flung areas, first implemented by the *Botika ng Barangay* (2001) program, which is currently implemented as the **Botika ng Bayan** (2018).
4. **Emergency Care**
5. **Maternal and Child Health**
6. **Adequate Food and Proper Nutrition**
7. **Locally Endemic Diseases**: (mn. *DeLeMa*) dengue, leptospirosis, leprosy, [^2]malaria
8. **Health Education**
9. **Communicable Diseases**: R.A. 11332 is a law on the mandatory reporting of notifiable diseases.

## Referral System
1. **Primary Referral System**: areas of first contact between the community members and healthcare practitioners. These include rural health units (RHUs), barangay health centers (BHCs), lying-in clinics, etc.
2. **Secondary Referral System**: areas of referral when primary-level facilities are unable to handle a client and the client requires hospitalization. These include provincial hospitals and district hospitals.
3. **Tertiary Referral System**: specialized, critical care facilities to handle clients who require care beyond the capabilities of a secondary facility. These include specialized centers such as the Lung Center of the Philippines, Philippine Heart Center, Philippine Orthopedic Center, National Center for Mental Health, etc.

# Department of Health
The Department of Health is the main health department of the Philippines.
- **Mission**: to promote healthy settings and stir the development of an effective, resilient, equitable, and people-centered universal health care.
- **Vision**: Filipinos are among the healthiest people in south-east asia by 2022.
- **Vision**: Filipinos are among the healthiest people in asia by 2040.

	1. Program Development
	2. Health Service Regulation

	1. Leadership in health
	2. Enablers and capacity builders
	3. Administrator of services
## Goals of the Department of Health
1. **Jose Marcelo "Erap" Ejercito**: Health Sector Reform Agenda
2. **Gloria Macapagal Arroyo**:
	- FOURmula One For Health, with four principles: health financing, health service delivery, health regulation, and good governance.
	- Project NARS (Nurses Assigned in Rural Service) are deployed to rural areas for six months on an allowance of 8,000 PHP per month; they are not employed.
3. **Benigno "PNoy" Aquino III**:
	- Universal Health Care, including six principles: the FOURmula One For Health (1-4), human resource for health (5), and health information (6).
	- Registered Nurses for Health Enhancement and Local Service (RN HEALS) Project is similar to Project NARS, in that deployment occurs. However, the nurses became officially employed, and the deployment period became extended to 12 months. A nurse's salary under this program remained at 8,000 PHP per month.
4. **Rodrigo Roa "Digong" Duterte**: Two secretaries of health appeared during this period, each with their own goals.
	- Dr. Ubial: "All for heatlh toward health for all"

	- Nurses Deployment Program amends RN HEALS, reducing the deployment period to 6 months but allowing for renewal. It also improved the nursing salary up to 30,000-33,000 PHP per month (Salary Grade 15-16), and gave these nurses a title of "Public Health Nurse II".
5. **Ferdinand "Bongbong" Marcos Jr.**:
	- Health Sector Strategy, "Sulong Kalusugan 2023-2028", outlined four "strategic thrusts":
		1. Strengthened health institutions and workforce, the priority thrust.
		2. Enable filipinos to be healthy
		3. Protect filipinos from health risks
		4. Care for filipinos health and wellness
	- The Department of Health Secretary (Dr. Teodoro Herbosa) identified five core values (mn. *PRICE*):
		1. Professionalism
		2. Reliability

## PhilHealth
**PhilHealth** is the implementing arm of the DOH. It is the national health insurance program. There are multiple policies governing the actions of PhilHealth:
1. **R.A. 7875**: The original PhilHealth law
2. **R.A. 10606**: The amended PhilHealth law (2013), updating the contribution to PhilHealth to 5% by 2025.

Individuals benefiting from PhilHealth are *beneficiaries*:
1. **Self-earning individuals**, i.e., businessmen.
2. **Sponsored individuals**, i.e., employees of businessmen.
3. **Retirees** may continue to contribute voluntarily.
4. **Overseas Filipino Workers** (OFWs) continue to contribute, whose benefits are used by their dependents rather than themselves. OFWs are protected by **R.A. 10022** (Migrant Workers Act)
5. **Indigents** are covered by **R.A. 11463**, the Malasakit Center Act.
6. **Abandoned children** are covered by PhilHealth.
7. **Senior Citizens** are covered by **R.A. 10645**, the Expanded Senior Citizen Act, which provides a free lifetime membership to PhilHealth. Contributions are no longer necessary towards PhilHealth.
8. **Persons with disability** (PWDs) are given mandatory PhilHealth coverage through **R.A. 11228**, "Giving Mandatory PhilHealth to PWDs"
9. **Cancer patients** are given mandatory PhilHealth coverage through **R.A. 11215**: "National Integrated Cancer Control Act", which cover costs including chemotherapeutic medications.
10. **Dependents**, such as retired parents, legitimate spouses, and children (<21 years old) may be enrolled as beneficiaries to a member's benefits.

The **benefits** of PhilHealth insurance include:
1. Rooms (confinement)
2. Prescription medications
3. Laboratory tests and diagnostic procedures
4. Operations and surgical procedures
5. Professional fees may be covered, but only to a maximum of 45 days of professional fees across all dependents of a PhilHealth member.

**Exclusions** of PhilHealth include:
1. Grand multiparous women, i.e., 5th pregnancies onwards.
2. Non-prescription drugs and devices (e.g., assistive devices).
3. Treatment for alcohol abuse.
4. Optometric services, e.g. glasses and contact lenses
5. Cosmetic surgeries

# Public Health Programs
## Micronutrient Supplementation Program
This program was put into place by **DOH A.O. 2010-0010**, the Micronutrient Supplementation Program, also known as "*Garantisadong Pambata*". This is done twice a year, in 6-month intervals: April and [^3]October. Supplementations include (mn. *AIIR123*):
1. **Vitamin A**:
	- <1 year old: 100,000 IU is given at 6 months of age (first dose).
	- >1 year old: 200,000 IU is given every 6 months until the child is 5 years old.

2. **Iodine**: 200 mg/cap, 1 cap per year.
3. **Iron**: [^4]30 mg/5 ml (1 tablespoon), given once daily for 3 months.

## Food Fortification Law
This program was put into place by **R.A. 8976**, the Food Fortification Law. This outlined standards for a food fortification program targeting staple foods of children, such as junk food. Additionally, products that met these standards were granted the *Sangkap Pinoy* seal.

## Kalusugan at Nutrisyon Ng Magnanay Act
This program was put into place by **R.A. 11148**, the "First 1000 Days Law". This outlined the nutritional requirements of a mother and her child from conception up to the second year of life. This includes various policies:
1. **E.O. 51**: The Milk Code, enforcing the benefits of breastfeeding for infants. Even the mention of a brand of formula is prohibited by this act.
2. **E.O. 10028**: Expanded Breastfeeding Promotion Act incentivized the presence of lactation stations in large establishments (e.g., malls, hotels).
3. **R.A. 8172**: Asin law applied iodine supplementation to salt as a countermeasure against iodine deficiency.
4. **R.A. 8976**: Food Fortification Law

## Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF)
A program outlining the prescribed dietary habits of infants and children.
1. **to 6 months**: exclusive breastfeeding
2. **after 6 months**: complementary feeding, as guided by four characteristics (mn. PAST):
	- **Properly-fed**: feeding is done when the infant shows signs of hunger.
	- **Adequate**: feeding should be proper.
	- **Safe**: food should be prepared hygieneically.
	- **Timely**: introduction of specific foods is based on the infant's age%% (mn. *C Vongvong at Ferdinand Marcos, Yawa*) %%.
		1. 7 months: **Cereals**, which are iron-fortified, as the infant begins to run out of iron stores obtained from the mother.
		2. 8 months: **Vegetables**
		3. 9 months: **Fruits** (pureed)
		4. 10 months: **Meat** (ground)
		5. 11 months: **Yolk of the egg**, which contains less allergens.
		6. 12 months: **White of the egg**

## Essential Intrapartum Newborn Care (EINC)
This program is based on **DOH A.O. 2009-0025**, also known as "*Unang Yakap*", four core steps done during the first few minutes of life of the newborn to improve the survival and health outcomes of delivery. These steps, chronologically, are:
1. **Immediate and thorough drying**
2. **Early skin-to-skin contact**
3. **Properly timed cord clamping and cutting**
4. **Rooming-in and early breastfeeding**, within 90 minutes

## Newborn Screening Act
This program is based on **R.A. 9288**. This traditionally aimed to detect six congenital metabolic disorders: G6PD, MSUD, Congenital Hypothyroidism, Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia, Galactosemia, and Phenyketonuria. Recently, an addendum by **DOH A.O. 2014-0045** created the *Expanded Newborn Screening Act*, which increases the detected diseases to 28 diseases. These included screening for hemoglobinopathies, amino acid disorders, organic acidurias, carbohydrate metabolism disorders, biotin metabolism, cystic fibrosis, endocrine disorders, and fatty acid oxidation disorders.

>[!WARNING] PhilHealth does not cover the expanded newborn screening act. Usually, expanded screening is performed in screening facilities.

>[!TIP] Principles of Newborn Screening
>1. Only **1 cc** of blood is necessary via heel prick.
>2. Samples are obtained after **48 to 72 hours**, because PKU cannot be detected if a sample is obtained in the first 24 hours. Another 24 hours is provided to ensure the provision of adequate nutrition prior to sample collection.
>3. Interpretation of findings:
>		- If the newborn results are positive, they proceed to confirmatory testing. Diagnosis is establish if a positive confirmatory result returns.
>		- If the newborn results are negative, no problems are expected.

## Expanded Program on Immunization
1. The first incarnation of this program began with **P.D. 996** in 1976, the National Immunization Program.
	- Children \<8 years old must receive compulsory basic immunization for seven diseases: tuberculosis, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, poliomyelitis, measles, rubella.
2. The second iteration of this program was **R.A. 7846** (, adding the hepatitis B vaccine to the previous act.
3. The latest amendment to the program is **R.A. 10152** (2011), the *Mandatory Infants and Children Health Immunization Act*. This added two more vaccines: mumps and *Haemophilus influenzae B* (HiB).
4. In 2012, an addendum is the inclusion of [^5]**rotavirus vaccine**.
5. In 2013, an addendum is the inclusion of **pneumococcal conjugated vaccine**.

# Levels of Health Care Facilities
1. **R.A. 4226**, the Hospital Licensure Act, outlines the standards and requirements that a hospital must meet prior to licensure by the Department of Health to operate.
2. **DOH A.O. 2012-0012**, the New DOH Hospital Classifications: Levels 1, 2, and 3.

Based on R.A. 4266, all hospitals must have:
1. Consultants for pediatric and adult patients, surgery, and obstetric patients.
2. Emergency room
3. Out-patient department
4. Isolation facility
5. Surgery and maternity facility
6. Dental services
7. Pharmacy
8. Laboratory

- **Special units** are *departmentalized* services, such as a cardiac unit, stroke unit, respiratory unit, high-risk maternity unit, and intensive care units (NICU, PICU).
- ***Other* services** are highly specialized units, such as a blood bank, dialysis units, rehabilitation units, ambulatory surgical unit, and residential training.

| Requirement        | Level 1  | Level 2    | Level 3      |
| ------------------ | -------- | ---------- | ------------ |
| **Number of Beds** | 25 to 75 | 100 to 200 | 200+         |
| **Special Units**  | None     | Present    | Present      |
| **Other Services** | None     | None       | At least one |

>[!EXAMPLE] Other Facility Types
>**Stand-alone facilities** are facilities existing outside of hospitals and can be classified into four groups:
>1. **Category A**: Primary care facilities; the first contact of the community. This includes health stations, health centers, lying-ins, dental clinics, etc.
>2. **Category B**: Custodial care facilities involves facilities that provide health needs for patients that are within the facility's *custody*--these are long-term care facilities, e.g., hospice centers, nursing homes, rehabilitation facilities, etc.
>3. **Category C**: Diagnostic and therapeutic facilities are testing facilities and laboratories, e.g., drug-testing sites, STD testing facilities, blood services facilities, etc.
>4. **Category D**: Specialized outpatient facilities that provide care often provided in the hospital, e.g., dialysis centers, chemotherapy-radiation facilities, and ambulatory surgery clinics.
