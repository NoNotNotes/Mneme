# Anemia
Anemia is a condition in which the blood lacks adequate healthy red blood cells or hemoglobin, with most common causes being acute blood loss, decreased or faulty red blood cell production (hypoproliferative anemia), or the destruction of red blood cells (hemolytic anemia). There are several types of anemia, with the main types being anemia related to acute and chronic blood loss, anemia of chronic diseases (including cancers, immunodeficiency syndrome, renal disease, liver diseases, and autoimmune conditions), anemias caused by nutritional deficiencies (such as iron, folate, or vitamin B₁₂ deficiency), and hereditary anemias (including sickle cell anemia and thalassemia).

>[!SUCCESS] Treatment of anemia varies, focusing on the causative factor.
>General interventions include (a) the administration of blood products and hematopoietic medications as prescribed to treat anemia related to acute and chronic conditions, (b) diet modification to relieve deficiencies that result in anemia, and (c) control and address the source of acute blood loss.

## Iron-Deficiency Anemia
Iron stores are depleted, resulting in a decreased iron supply for the manufacture of hemoglobin in red blood cells. This commonly results from blood loss, increased metabolic demands, syndromes of gastrointestinal malabsorption, and dietary inadequacy. Older clients often have poor diets or chewing difficulties that place them at risk for anemias.
1. **Assessment**: Pallor, weakness and fatigue; low hemoglobin, low hematocrit, low mean cellular volume (MCV) levels; and microcytic and hypochromic red blood cells.
2. **Interventions**:
	- Increase oral intake of iron; administer iron supplements as prescribed.
	- Intramuscular (via Z-track) or intravascular (if severe) administration of iron.
	- Teach clients how to administer iron supplements. To maximize absorption, an acidic environment is preferred—take iron in between meals and with vitamin C (from a multivitamin or fruit juice such as tomato juice). Do not take iron with milk or antacids, because these decrease absorption.
	- Teach clients about the side effects of iron supplements: black stools, constipation, and foul aftertaste. Also inform the client about the use of a straw to take liquid iron due to its ability to stain the teeth. The client should brush their teeth after administration.

## Vitamin B₁₂-Deficiency Anemia
A macrocytic (megaloblastic) anemia that results from an inadequate intake of vitamin B₁₂ or lack of absorption of ingested vitamin B₁₂ from the intestinal tract (pernicious anemia). The large, underdeveloped red blood cells can break down, resulting in severe pallor and possible jaundice.
1. **Assessment**: Severe pallor, fatigue, weight loss, smooth, beefy red tongue, slight jaundice, paresthesias of the hand and feet, disturbances with gait and balance.
	- Neurological involvement (paresthesias, ataxia) is not directly related to the anemia, but rather to the deficiency of vitamin B₁₂, which is related to nerve and brain health.
2. **Interventions**:
	- Increase dietary intake of foods rich in vitamin B₁₂, such as meat and liver, if the anemia is a result of dietary deficiency.
	- Administer vitamin B₁₂ injections as prescribed, weekly initially and then monthly for maintenance (lifelong) if the anemia is a result of deficiency of intrinsic factor or disease or surgery of the ileum.
	- Oral B₁₂ preparations and nasal spray or sublingual cobalamin (B₁₂) preparations may be prescribed to maintain vitamin level.

>[!WARNING] Pernicious Anemia
>Pernicious anemia results from a deficiency of intrinsic factor, normally secreted by the gastric mucosa), necessary for intestinal absorption of vitamin B₁₂; gastric disease or surgery (e.g., Billroth I or II procedures) can result in a lack of intrinsic factor.

## Folate-Deficiency Anemia
A macrocytic (megaloblastic) anemia in which red blood cells are larger than normal and are oval-shaped rather than round-shaped due to the lack of inadequate intake of folate (vitamin B₉). Folic acid is required for red blood cell formation and maturation. Common causes include dietary deficiency; malabsorption syndromes such as celiac disease, Crohn's disease, or small bowel resection; medications (such as antiseizure medications) that decrease the absorption of folic acid; a condition (including pregnancy) that increases the requirement of folic acid; chronic alcoholism; and chronic hemodialysis.
1. **Assessment**: Dyspepsia; smooth, beefy red tongue; pallor, fatigue, and weakness; tinnitus; tachycardia.
2. **Interventions**:
	- Encourage the client to eat foods rich in folic acid, such as green leafy vegetables, meat, liver, fish, legumes, peanuts, orange juice, and avocado.
	- Administer folic acid as prescribed.
## Sickle Cell Anemia
Sickle cell anemia constitutes a group of diseases termed hemoglobinopathies, in which hemoglobin A (HgA) is partly or completely replaced by abnormal sickle hemoglobin S (HgS). It is caused by the inheritance of a gene for a structurally abnormal portion of the hemoglobin chain.
1. **Risk Factors**: Parents heterozygous for HgS or being of African American descent.
2. **Screening Test**: The sickle turbidity test (Sickledex) is frequently used because it can be performed on blood from a fingerstick and yields accurate results in 3 minutes.
3. **Differential Test**: If the sickle turbidity test is positive, testing with hemoglobin electrophoresis is necessary to distinguish between children with the trait and those with the disease.
4. **Pathophysiology**: Hemoglobin S is sensitive to changes in the oxygen content of the red blood cell. Insufficient oxygen causes the cells to assume a sickle shape (*sickling*), and the cells become rigid and clumped together, obstructing capillary blood flow. Obstruction is the primary cause of clinical manifestations. Sickling also results in increased red blood cell destruction.
	- Situations that precipitate sickling include **fever, dehydration, and emotional or physical stress**; any condition that increases the need for oxygen or alters the transport of oxygen can result in sickle cell crisis (acute exacerbation).
	- Sickle cell crises are acute exacerbations of the disease, which vary considerably in severity and frequency; these include vaso-occlusive crisis, splenic sequestration, hyperhemolytic crisis, and aplastic crisis.
	- The sickling response is reversible under conditions of adequate oxygenation and hydration; after repeated sickling, the cell becomes permanently sickled.
5. **Sickle Cell Crises**:

| Crisis                    | Cause                                                                                                                       | Manifestations                                                                           |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Vaso-Occlusive Crisis** | Stasis of blood with clumping of cells in the microcirculation, ischemia, and infarction                                    | Fever; painful swelling of the hands, feet, joints, or affected area; and abdominal pain |
| **Splenic Sequestration** | Pooling and clumping of blood in the spleen (hypersplenism)                                                                 | Profound anemia, hypovolemia, and shock                                                  |
| **Hyperhemolytic Crisis** | Accelerated rate of red blood cell destruction over a period of time                                                        | Anemia, jaundice, and reticulocytosis                                                    |
| **Aplastic Crisis**       | Diminished production and increased destruction of red blood cells, triggered by viral infection or depletion of folic acid | Profound anemia and pallor                                                               |

6. **Interventions**:
	- **Maintain adequate hydration** and blood flow through oral and intravenously (IV) administered fluids. Electrolyte replacement is also provided as needed; without adequate hydration, pain will not be controlled.
	- Administer oxygen and blood transfusions as prescribed to increase tissue perfusion; exchange transfusions, which reduce the number of circulating sickle cells and the risk of complications, may also be prescribed.
	- Administer analgesics as prescribed (around the clock).
	- Assist the child to assume a comfortable position so that the child keeps the extremities extended to promote venous return; elevate the head of the bed no more than 30 degrees, avoid putting strain on painful joints, and do not raise the knee gatch of the bed.
	- Encourage consumption of a high-calorie, high-protein diet, with folic acid supplementation.
	- Administration of hydroxyurea, an antimetabolite, which helps to prevent the formation of sickle-shaped red blood cells and to decrease the incidence of vaso-occlusive events.
	-  Administer antibiotics as prescribed to prevent infection.
	- Monitor for signs of complications, including increasing anemia, decreased perfusion, and shock (mental status changes, pallor, vital sign changes).
	- Instruct the child and parents about the early signs and symptoms of crisis and the measures to prevent crisis.
	- Ensure that the child receives pneumococcal and meningococcal vaccines and an annual influenzas vaccine, because of susceptibility to infection secondary to functional asplenia.
	- A splenectomy may be necessary for clients who experience recurrent splenic sequestration.
	- Inform parents of the hereditary aspects of the disorder.

## Aplastic Anemia
Aplastic anemia is a deficiency of circulating erythrocytes and all other formed elements of blood, resulting from the arrested development of cells within the bone marrow. It can be primary (present at birth) or secondary (acquired). Several possible causes exist, including chronic exposure to myelotoxic agents, viruses and infections such as hepatitis, Epstein-Barr virus, autoimmune disorders such as human immunodeficiency virus, and allergy states.
1. The definitive diagnosis is determined by bone marrow aspiration (shows conversion of red bone marrow to fatty bone marrow).
2. Therapeutic management focuses on restoring function to the bone marrow and involves **immunosuppressive therapy** and **bone marrow transplantation**.
	- If the cause is a myelotoxic medication that is being administered for another purpose, the medication may be discontinued to improve bone marrow function.
3. **Assessment**: Pancytopenia (deficiency of erythrocytes, leukocytes, and thrombocytes). Bleeding tendencies include petechiae, purpura and bleeding. Anemia itself manifests as pallor, weakness, tachycardia, and fatigue.
4. **Interventions**:
	- Prepare the client for bone marrow transplantation if planned.
	- Administer immunosuppressive medications as prescribed; antilymphocytic globulin or antithymocyte globulin may be prescribed to suppress the autoimmune response.
	- Colony-stimulating factors may be prescribed to enhance bone marrow production.
	- Corticosteroids and cyclosporine may be prescribed.
	- Administer blood transfusions if prescribed and monitor for transfusion reactions. Note that transfusions could increase the chance for immune reactions to platelets and shorten the life span of transfused cells. This therapy is discontinued as soon as the bone marrow begins to produce RBCs if the problem is transient.

# Hemophilia
*Hemophilia* refers to a group of bleeding disorders resulting from a deficiency of specific coagulation proteins. Identifying the specific coagulation deficiency is important so that definitive treatment with the specific **replacement agent** can be implemented; aggressive replacement therapy is initiated to prevent the chronic crippling effects from joint bleeding.
1. The most common types are factor VIII deficiency (hemophilia A or classic hemophilia) and factor IX deficiency (hemophilia B or Christmas disease).
