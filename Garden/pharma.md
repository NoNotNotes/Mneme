# Antihypertensive Drugs
Drugs used for patients with hypertension whose therapeutic target is a decrease in blood pressure. This may be achieved through various means such as decreasing circulating blood volume, decreasing heart contractility, producing vasodilation, among others.

>[!WARNING] General Side Effects
>The nature of antihypertensive drugs will result in two (often) universal side effects:
>1. **Orthostatic hypotension**, a drop in blood pressure associated with changes in positioning from supine to sitting to standing.
>2. **Rebound hypertension**, a return of hypertension after cessation of therapy.

## Angiotensin Converting Enzyme Inhibitors and Angiotensin Receptor Blockers
Angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) both act upon the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system to reduce its effects on blood pressure.

>[!INFO] Pertinent Physiology
>The **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System** (RAA System) is a mechanism triggered by hypotension that stimulates the release of renin, which interacts with angiotensinogen produced by the liver to produce angiotensin I. Angiotensin I is converted to angiotensin II by angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) produced by the lungs, and is the most potent vasodilator in the body. This acts to increase blood pressure, alter renal functioning, and increase circulating volume (see below).

1. **Persistent dry cough** is related to the accumulation of bradykinin and substance P—inflammatory substances that are broken down by ACE. When ACE is inhibited, these accumulate and irritate the airway mucosa, resulting in persistent, non-productive coughing.
	- This may be a contraindication for patients with respiratory problems.
	- Patients may be switched to ARBs, which do not affect bradykinin and substance P.
2. **Hyperkalemia** can result in dysrhythmias and ECG changes (Peaked T waves, wide QRS, prolonged PR-interval). Monitor serum enzymes regularly and alter dietary potassium intake. Avoid potassium-rich food and potassium-saving drugs.
3. **Angioedema** involves rapid swelling of the face and upper airway, also related to inflammatory substances. Inflammation produces vasodilation, which increases permeability, allowing fluids to leak into tissue. The primary problem that arises is airway obstruction due to soft tissue swelling, as manifested by difficulty of breathing, wheezes, or stridor. Oxygenation and supportive airways may become necessary.
4. **Renal impairment** may occur if ACE inhibitors are given to patients with severe heart failure. This occurs due to renal hypoperfusion resulting in prerenal acute kidney injury (AKI).
5. **Teratogenesis** can occur if ACE inhibitors are given to pregnant patients. This happens as the fetal renin-angiotensin system, which plays a crucial role in renal development and blood pressure regulation in fetal life, becomes disrupted. Renal perfusion drops, tubulogenesis is impaired, fetal hypotension occurs, and urinary output is [^1]reduced.

## Alpha Adrenergic Blockers

>[!INFO] Pertinent Physiology
>**Adrenergic agents** act by stimulating the five types of receptors found in the sympathetic nervous system. These vary in their region of effect, which include:
>1. **Alpha-1 Receptors**: affects all organs affected by the sympathetic nervous system except the heart. Its primary targets are **blood vessels**, producing vasoconstriction.
>2. **Alpha-2 Receptors**: a modulator; prevents overstimulation by exerting opposing effects. Overall, this reduces blood pressure and causes hypotension.
>3. **Beta-1 Receptors**: affects the heart and kidneys, producing tachycardia of the heart and fluid retention and increased blood pressure through the release of **renin**, part of the RAA system.
>4. **Beta-2 Receptors**: affects the smooth muscles, particularly of the respiratory tract, producing bronchodilation to improve airflow. It also affects other organs such as the stomach to reduce peristalsis.
>5. **Beta-3 Receptors**: lipolysis and relaxation of the bladder.
>
>Further discussion on the use of adrenergic agonists and antagonists are discussed in [[#Drugs Affecting the Autonomic Nervous System]]

# Drugs Affecting the Autonomic Nervous System

**Sympathetic** (catecholamines; norepinephrine, epinephrine) vs **parasympathetic** (acetylcholine) Stimulation
1. **Eyes**: pupillary dilation mydriasis vs constriction miosis
2. **Heart**: tachycardia vs bradycardia
3. **Airways**: bronchodilation vs bronchoconstriction
4. **GI**: hypomotility vs hypermotility
5. **Bladder**: relaxation (urinary retention) vs contraction (voiding)

>[!DRUG] Pertinent Physiology
>**Adrenergic agents** (*"adrene-" from adrenaline*), refers to drugs that affect the sympathetic system which runs on adrenaline. These act by stimulating the five types of receptors found in the sympathetic nervous system. These vary in their region of effect:
>- **Alpha-1 Receptors**: affects all organs affected by the sympathetic nervous system except the heart. Its primary targets are **blood vessels**, producing vasoconstriction.
>- **Alpha-2 Receptors**: a modulator; prevents overstimulation by exerting opposing effects. Overall, this reduces blood pressure and causes hypotension.
>- **Beta-1 Receptors**: affects the heart and kidneys, producing tachycardia of the heart and fluid retention and increased blood pressure through the release of **renin**, part of the RAA system.
>- **Beta-2 Receptors**: affects the smooth muscles, particularly of the respiratory tract, producing bronchodilation to improve airflow. It also affects other organs such as the stomach to reduce peristalsis.
>- **Beta-3 Receptors**: lipolysis and relaxation of the bladder.
>
>**Cholinergic agents** (*"choline-" from acetylcholine*) refers to drugs that affect the parasympathetic system which runs on acetylcholine.
>- **Nicotinic Receptors**: receptors that have a high affinity for nicotine. These are found in skeletal muscles and are situated pre-ganglionic in neurons. Stimulation of nicotinic receptors is non-selective; it can produce both sympathetic and parasympathetic effects due to its location in the pre-ganglion.
>- **Muscarinic Receptors**: the stronger of the two receptors, with high affinity for muscarine (often found in poisonous mushrooms). Contrastingly, these are post-ganglionic, limiting its effects to those of the parasympathetic system. This makes it more appealing as a target for pharmacologic intervention. Thus, this is the focus of this discussion. There are five further divisions of muscarinic receptors:
>		- **M1**: affects the CNS
>		- **M2**: affects the heart
>		- **M3**: affects smooth muscles; most areas besides the heart
>		- **M4**: affects the CNS
>		- **M5**: affects the CNS

## Adrenergic Agonists
**Adrenergic agonists** are those that increase the sympathetic response either non-selectively or selectively (which receptors are activated). As discussed above, this either targets Alpha 1 (increases BP), Alpha 2 (decreases BP), Beta 1 (increases HR), and Beta 2 (causes bronchodilation).

|**Category**|**Drugs**|**Primary Use(s)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Selective Alpha Adrenergic Agonist**|Phenylephrine|Increases blood pressure in hypotension; nasal decongestant (via vasoconstriction)|
|**Non-selective Adrenergic Agonist**|Epinephrine, Dopamine|Used for shock|
|**Selective Alpha-1 Adrenergic Agonist**|Pseudoephedrine, Oxymetazoline|Used for hypotension|
|**Selective Alpha-2 Adrenergic Agonist**|Clonidine (Catapres), Methyldopa, Midodrine|Reduces blood pressure|
|**Non-selective Beta Adrenergic Agonist**|Isoproterenol|Increases HR, bronchodilation; used for asthma, dysrhythmia, heart failure|
|**Selective Beta-1 Adrenergic Agonist**|Dobutamine|Used in heart failure and some dysrhythmias to increase heart rate|
|**Selective Beta-2 Adrenergic Agonist**|Albuterol, Salmeterol, Formoterol, Metaproterenol, Terbutaline|Bronchodilators|
|**Selective Beta-3 Adrenergic Agonist**|Droxidopa, Mirabegron|Used for urinary frequency|

>[!TIP] Nursing Considerations
>With the use of sympathetic drugs, the nurse must monitor **vital signs**, especially those affected by the medication.
>- **Urine output** and **cardiac output** must also be assessed.
>- **Breathing patterns** should be checked.
>- **Check for photosensitivity**, as sympathetic agonists will produce mydriasis, which can result in light sensitivity.
>- **Monitor for rhinorrhea** especially during the use of phenylephrine. Epistaxis may also occur.

## Adrenergic Antagonists
**Adrenergic antagonists** block the sympathetic response from exerting its effects. Primarily, these are used for lowering blood pressure.
1. Non-selective Alpha Blockers: **Phentolamine**: used for severe hypertension, and at times for benign prostate hyperplasia (BPH).
2. Selective Alpha-1 Blockers: "*-zosin*"s; **Prazosin**, **Alfuzosin**, **Doxazosin**, **Terazosin**, **Tamsulosin**: used for hypertension.
3. Non-selective Beta Blockers: "-*olol*"s; **Nadolol**, **Carteolol**, **Propranolol**, **Timolol** (also suppresses aqueous humor production for glaucoma), **Sotalol**: slows the heart rate and produces bronchoconstriction. This may be used for angina.
4. Selective Beta-1 Blockers: "-*olol*"s; **Acebutolol**, **Atenolol**, **Bisoprolol**, **Metoprolol**, **Esmolol**: decreases heart rate, alleviating hypertension.

>[!TIP] Nursing Considerations
>With the use of sympathetic blockers, the nurse must monitor **vital signs**, especially those affected by the medication.
>- **Urine output** must be assessed. Urinary hesitancy, incomplete bladder emptying, and weak urination may occur.
>- **Check for orientation and LOC**. Decreased perfusion to the brain may express itself in dizziness or drowsiness.
>- Check **cardiac output** for adequacy.

## Cholinergic Stimulants
**Cholinergic Stimulants** promote this response through two primary methods: **direct-acting** stimulants and **indirect-acting** stimulants. The difference lies in whether the drug stimulates the receptors for acetylcholine through mimicry (direct) or blocks the effect of acetylcholinesterase or reuptake to increase its concentration (indirect).
1. **Direct-acting**:
	- **Urecholine** (Bethanicol): increases the parasympathetic response to the digestive and urinary tract, improving excretion through hypermotility and contraction. This is used in constipation and neurogenic urinary retention.
2. **Indirect-acting**:
	- "-*stigmines*"; **Physostigmine** (Antlirium). These contract skeletal muscles and increases blood flow to the CNS. This is used as an antidote for cholinergic crises such as in overdosing on anticholinergics.

>[!TIP] Nursing Considerations
>With the use of cholinergic stimulants, the nurse must **monitor for the possibility of a cholinergic crisis**. This produces an excess of parasympathetic responses, which result in **hypersalivation**, **miotic pupils**, **twitching**/**spasms** of skeletal muscles, **diaphoresis**, **difficulty of breathing** (bronchoconstriction), **hypermotility**, and **urinary frequency**. These are all effects of parasympathetic stimulation in excess.
>
>Additionally, **monitor liver function**. Cholinergics are often hepatotoxic. Liver enzymes are checked to identify early liver damage.
>
>The antidote used for cholinergic crisis is **atropine**.

## Cholinergic Blockers (Anticholinergics)
**Cholinergic Blockers** (Anticholinergics) inhibit the parasympathetic response. This allows sympathetic effects to take hold, such as mydriasis, increased heart rate, decreased gastrointestinal motility, bronchodilation, urinary retention, and decreases blood flow to the central nervous system (useful for Parkinson's). Indications include: peptic ulcer disease, ophthalmic examination, heart abnormalities (e.g., heart failure), asthma, and an overactive bladder.
1. **Bronchodilator Cholinergic Blockers**: "-*tropium*"s; **Ipratropium** (Atrovent), **Aclidinium**, and **Tiotropium**. These focus their effects on the lungs, allowing it to dilate.
2. **Chronotropic Cholinergic Blockers**: **Atropine**, **Glycopyrrolate**. Atropine is the more common form given, often as pre-operative medications to counteract the downer effects of anesthetics given during surgery. It also produces dry mouth and decreases GI secretions in order to prevent aspiration precautions.
3. **Urinary Retention Cholinergic Blockers**: **Oxybutinine**, **Telterodine**, **Fesoterodine**, **Darifenacin**, **Solifenacin**, **Trospium**. These are used for patients with urinary frequency.
4. **Mydriatic Cholinergic Blockers**: **Cyclopentolate**, **Tropicamide**
5. **Gastrointestinal Cholinergic Blockers**: **Scopalamine**, **Methscopalamine**, **Dicyclomine**, **Propantheline**. These are used to reduce peristalsis such as in cases of PUD.

>[!TIP] Nursing Considerations
>With the use of cholinergic blockers, the nurse must **monitor for the possibility of an anticholinergic crisis**. This produces an excess of sympathetic responses, which result in **dry mouth**, **hot dry skin**, **decreased GI motility**, **vision problems** due to mydriasis, and **decreased LOC** in severe cases.
>- The antidote used for anticholinergic crisis is **physostigmine**.
>- Provide ice cubes or hard candy for dry mouth.
>- Avoid strenuous activities and hot temperatures.
>- Monitor urine output.
>- Monitor heart rhythms
>- Watch out for abdominal distention due to gastrointestinal hypomotility
